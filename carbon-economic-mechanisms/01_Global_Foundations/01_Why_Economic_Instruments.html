Why Put a Price on Carbon

Before any mechanism — before Article 6, before an ETS, before a single credit — there is one economic argument. Understand it properly and every later design choice stops looking arbitrary: the instruments differ because governments are choosing what they want certainty about.

1

The missing cost

A coal plant's cost sheet is honest about coal, labour, capital, and maintenance. It is silent about the climate damage of the CO₂ leaving the stack — not because anyone is lying, but because no one sends an invoice for that damage. The cost is real; it simply lands on people who were not party to the transaction.

This is an externality, and it produces a specific, predictable failure: because the plant's private cost is lower than the social cost, the plant rationally emits more than is collectively optimal. Every actor behaves correctly and the aggregate outcome is still wrong.

The purpose of carbon pricing is not to punish emitters. It is to repair a bookkeeping error.

Put the missing cost onto the ledger, and the same profit-maximising behaviour that previously produced excess emissions starts producing abatement instead. Nothing about the firm's motivation has to change — only its cost function.

The two classical answers, and why both survive in today's architecture

Pigou (1920) argued the state should levy a tax equal to the marginal external damage, so the private cost rises to meet the social cost. That lineage runs directly to today's carbon tax and to Indonesia's Pungutan Atas Karbon.1

Coase (1960) argued that if property rights over the externality are clearly defined and bargaining is cheap, the parties will trade their way to the efficient outcome without the state setting a price at all — the state's job is to define and enforce the right. That lineage runs to cap-and-trade: an allowance is a property right in emissions.2

Neither displaced the other. Modern systems run both simultaneously, which is exactly why Perpres 110 defines a levy and an allowance market as parallel NEK instruments rather than alternatives.

Four levers, not two

“Carbon pricing vs regulation” is a false binary. A government picking an instrument is choosing from four families, and real systems combine them.

Read the middle two columns as a pair and the whole architecture snaps into focus: a price instrument and a quantity instrument are the same trade made in opposite directions. One fixes price and lets emissions float; the other fixes emissions and lets price float. You cannot fix both — that is not a policy failing, it is arithmetic.

3

Why a price beats a mandate

This is the single argument that justifies the existence of carbon markets. If you take one thing from Session 01, take this.

Abatement does not cost the same everywhere. Replacing worn industrial motors can save money outright; retrofitting CCS onto an existing coal unit is among the most expensive things you can do. Between those extremes sits everything else.

Now suppose a government mandates that every plant cut emissions by the same 20%. It has just forced the expensive plant to buy costly abatement while cheap abatement at the plant next door goes undone. Same tonnes, far higher national bill.

A carbon market does not reduce emissions. The cap does that. The market reduces the cost of the reduction.

Set the price, and every firm abates up to the point where its own next tonne costs more than the price — and pays for the rest. Cheap abatement everywhere gets used before expensive abatement anywhere. That is the whole efficiency argument, and it is why the same tonnage is delivered for less money than any mandate could achieve.

The formal version — the equimarginal principle

Total abatement cost is minimised when the marginal abatement cost is equal across all sources. A uniform mandate equalises quantities abated, which guarantees unequal marginal costs and therefore guarantees waste. A price equalises marginal costs and lets quantities differ — which is the condition for the cheapest possible delivery of a given target.3

Tradable permits reach the same point by a different route: firms with high abatement costs buy allowances from firms with low ones, and trading continues until no further mutually profitable trade exists — which is precisely where marginal costs have equalised. Coase's insight, mechanised.

Why the negative-cost measures matter more than they look

Measures 1–3 pay for themselves and are still not done. That apparent paradox — money left on the table — is well documented in the energy-efficiency literature, and it shapes what a carbon price can and cannot achieve in this segment. The barriers are not price: they are split incentives (the landlord buys the chiller, the tenant pays the bill), capital rationing, information cost, and the absence of clear internal ownership.

Consequence for instrument design: a carbon price alone will not unlock negative-cost abatement, because those measures were already profitable and were skipped anyway. Efficiency needs the fourth lever — obligation, finance, or information — not the second or third. Any policy package that relies on price alone leaves the cheapest tonnes on the table.

4

Price or quantity — how a government actually chooses

Both instruments reach the efficient outcome if the government knows every firm's abatement costs. It never does. Once you admit that uncertainty, the two stop being equivalent — and the choice becomes principled rather than ideological.

Weitzman's rule — the actual decision criterion

Weitzman (1974) showed the choice turns on the relative slopes of the marginal damage and marginal abatement cost curves under uncertainty.4

If marginal damage is steep — one extra tonne does sharply more harm — getting the quantity wrong is costly, so use a quantity instrument. If marginal abatement cost is steep — a small overshoot in required abatement gets very expensive very fast — getting the price wrong is costly, so use a price instrument.

For CO₂ specifically the standard reading is that it is a stock pollutant: damage depends on cumulative atmospheric concentration, so the marginal damage of one particular year's tonne is comparatively flat. On Weitzman's criterion that argues for a price instrument. In practice governments overwhelmingly built quantity instruments anyway — because a cap is what you can put in a treaty pledge, and “tax” is what loses elections. The theory points one way and the politics the other, which is why hybrids won.

Absolute vs intensity — the fork you asked about

Same instrument, two ways to write the obligation. The difference is invisible in a policy summary and decisive in a growing economy.

Absolute: a hard tonnage. “Emit no more than X tonnes.” Intensity: emissions per unit of output. “Emit no more than X tonnes per MWh / per tonne of cement.” An intensity target rewards efficiency but places no ceiling on total emissions — because it scales with production.

Where this lands for Indonesia

Perpres 110/2025 defines Alokasi Karbon as “jumlah emisi karbon dioksida ekuivalen (CO₂e) yang diizinkan selama periode waktu tertentu sesuai kapasitas nasional” — an absolute quantity of CO₂e permitted over a period.5 The national frame is therefore absolute, and it is the stated basis for the NDC itself (Pasal 6, p.9).

Indonesia's power-sector trading scheme has been reported to operate on intensity benchmarks at installation level. [?] — to be verified against the implementing ministerial regulation in Session 08 rather than asserted here.

If both hold, the architecture contains a deliberate translation problem: intensity-based obligations at the installation, an absolute allocation at the nation. That translation — not the trading mechanics — is where the real design difficulty sits, and it is worth watching for in every implementing rule we read from here on.

6

Why no real system is a pure form

Every mature system bolts price features onto quantity instruments, because pure forms fail in predictable ways.

The three standard bolt-ons

Price floor — a reserve price at auction, or a top-up tax. Stops the price collapsing when the cap turns out to be loose or a recession cuts demand. Without one, an ETS can deliver its tonnage target and still send no investment signal at all.

Price ceiling / cost-containment reserve — extra allowances released if the price spikes. Protects industry from a cost shock, at the cost of loosening the cap in exactly the year it was binding.

Banking and borrowing — carry allowances forward (or draw them back). Smooths prices across years and lets firms time abatement investment sensibly, rather than optimising to an arbitrary compliance date.

Indonesia's version, and why it is not quite the same thing

Karbon Cadangan — reserve carbon — is defined as carbon allocated at national level for risk control in achieving the NDC target (Pasal 1(17), p.4).5 Perpres 110 also requires that Alokasi Karbon be set taking it into account (Pasal 3(3), p.8).

Note the stated purpose carefully: it is a buffer against the country missing its target, not primarily a market price-stabilisation tool. A cost-containment reserve protects firms from price spikes; Karbon Cadangan protects the state from NDC failure. They may end up being used similarly, but they are justified differently — and if the reserve is ever released into the market, which purpose governs will matter a great deal. An open question for Session 08.

“Compliance vs market priority”

You heard this phrase and it is genuinely ambiguous — it gets used for two different debates. Both are worth holding separately.

Reading A — the mitigation hierarchy

Should a regulated firm be allowed to buy its way out, or must it cut its own emissions first? Nearly every compliance system answers “cut first, offset the residual”, and enforces it with a quantitative limit on how much of an obligation may be met with offset credits rather than own-abatement or allowances.

The reasoning: unlimited offset use lets the cap be met on paper while the regulated sector never transforms — and since offsets are usually cheaper than deep sectoral abatement, an unlimited system would buy the cheapest tonnes forever and never build the capital stock the transition requires.

Reading B — which market a country builds first

A compliance market creates guaranteed demand, but needs legislation, an MRV system, a registry, and a regulator — years of work, and it makes enemies. A voluntary market can be stood up much faster, but its demand is discretionary, thin, and vulnerable to integrity shocks.

The trap in sequencing voluntary-first: projects get built against a demand curve that can evaporate, and the country establishes prices and expectations that the later compliance system inherits. Indonesia has been building both tracks in parallel — Perpres 110 defines domestic trading, international transfer, offsets, RBP and the levy as one instrument family rather than a sequence.5

Full treatment in Session 05 (compliance beyond ETS) and Session 06 (VCM). Flagged here only so the phrase stops being ambiguous when you next hear it.

8

The same choice, three lenses

Government

Choosing an instrument is choosing what you want certainty about — and what you can politically survive. A cap buys you a defensible NDC number; a levy buys you revenue and administrative simplicity. Ask of any proposal: what does this guarantee, and what has it left floating?

Project developer

Your revenue is a policy artifact, not a market discovery. Under a cap, demand for your credits exists because the state made it scarce; under a levy, it may not exist at all. Diligence the instrument that creates your buyer before you diligence the buyer.

Project / asset owner

Instrument type sets your revenue volatility profile. Cap-and-trade means a floating price you cannot hedge far out; a levy means a stable administered price with no upside. If your model uses a flat carbon price for fifteen years, it is describing a levy — check that is the instrument you are actually in.

Energy and forestry meet the theory differently

Your two sectors sit at opposite ends of what these instruments handle well — which explains, before any Indonesian specifics, why they ended up in different mechanisms.

Comprehension check

Five questions. Answer in your own words before Session 02; if any is uncomfortable, say which and we will re-cut that part.

  1. A minister says “our ETS will guarantee both a stable carbon price and our 2030 tonnage target.” What is wrong with that sentence, and which of the two promises would you expect to break first?
