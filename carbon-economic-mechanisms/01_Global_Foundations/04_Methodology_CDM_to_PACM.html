Methodologies and the Crediting Cycle

A methodology is the machine that converts a physical act — a turbine spinning, a boiler not firing — into a serialised financial instrument. This session opens the machine. I fetched and read the actual Article 6.4 rulebook for it, so every claim below carries a paragraph number, and both flags left open in Session 03 are now closed.

2

Baseline — the rulebook gives exactly three routes

Session 00 introduced the baseline as the number that determines credit volume. Here is how Article 6.4 constrains it — and note how much stricter it is than the CDM instinct of “projected business as usual”.

Para. 33 sets the overarching quality bar for every methodology: it must be “real, transparent, conservative, credible, below ‘business as usual’”, avoid leakage, recognise suppressed demand, align to the Paris temperature goal, and contribute to reducing emission levels in the host Party.1

“Below business as usual” is the phrase that separates PACM from the CDM.

A CDM baseline aimed to represent business as usual. A 6.4 baseline must sit underneath it. Every credit is therefore discounted by construction — the mechanism is designed so that crediting alone tightens the counterfactual over time.

Route (para. 36)How the baseline is setWhere it fits your sectors
36(a)(i)
Performance-based		 Based on best available technologies that represent an economically feasible and environmentally sound course of action Strong fit for new-build power and industrial equipment, where a defensible technology frontier exists
36(a)(ii)
Ambitious benchmark		 Set at least at the average emission level of the best performing comparable activities providing similar outputs in a defined scope and similar circumstances The natural route for industrial energy efficiency — it is the same logic as an ETS intensity benchmark, so the two can be made consistent
36(a)(iii)
Historical, adjusted		 Existing actual or historical emissions, adjusted downwards to align with para. 33 Common where no comparable fleet exists; the downward adjustment is the safeguard against grandfathering poor performance

Two further provisions matter commercially. Para. 36 closes by noting the host Party may determine a more ambitious level at its discretion — sovereign tightening is expressly permitted. And para. 37 allows standardized baselines, developed by the Supervisory Body at a host Party's request or by the host Party and approved centrally, “established at the highest possible level of aggregation”.

Why standardized baselines are the quiet efficiency win

Session 00 flagged MRV as the dominant recurring cost, and noted it scales badly for small projects. A standardized baseline moves the counterfactual argument from the project to the sector: it is settled once, centrally, for everyone. Each project then inherits it rather than paying consultants to re-derive it.

For Indonesia this is the highest-leverage thing a government can do for a small-project pipeline — industrial efficiency, small hydro, biogas — because it removes the fixed cost that makes those projects uneconomic to certify individually. Worth watching whether the implementing regulations take it up.

3

Additionality — the definition Session 00 could not find

Perpres 110 never defines it. The Article 6.4 rulebook does, in one dense paragraph, and it is stricter than the folk definition.

Para. 38 — “Additionality shall be demonstrated using a robust assessment that shows the activity would not have occurred in the absence of the incentives from the mechanism, taking into account all relevant national policies, including legislation, and representing mitigation that exceeds any mitigation that is required by law or regulation, and taking a conservative approach that avoids locking in levels of emissions, technologies or carbon-intensive practices…”1

Three tests are packed in there, and projects fail on different ones:

TestThe questionTypical failure
CounterfactualWould this have happened without carbon revenue?A project already financed and under construction, retro-fitted with a carbon story
Regulatory
surplus		Does it exceed what law or regulation already requires?Claiming credit for meeting a mandatory efficiency standard or a renewable portfolio obligation
Lock-in
avoidance		Does crediting it entrench a carbon-intensive path?Efficiency credits on a coal unit that extend its economic life

The regulatory-surplus test is the one that bites in Indonesia

Because additionality must account for all relevant national policies, including legislation, an ambitious domestic policy destroys the additionality of projects that merely comply with it. If a regulation mandates a technology, deploying that technology can no longer be credited.

This creates a genuine and underappreciated tension for a government: the more you regulate, the smaller your creditable pipeline becomes. It is not a reason to regulate less — it is a reason to design carbon policy and sectoral regulation together, and to expect the creditable universe to shrink as regulation matures. Carry this into Session 10, where it directly shapes what is creditable in Indonesian power and industry.

The one flexibility the rulebook grants

Para. 39 permits the Supervisory Body to apply simplified additionality approaches for any least developed country or small island developing State at that Party's request. Indonesia is neither, so the standard demonstration applies — a small but real competitive asymmetry against LDC-hosted projects.

Renewal is a re-test, not a rubber stamp

Para. 57: renewing a crediting period requires approval by both the Supervisory Body and the host Party, following a technical assessment by a DOE to determine necessary updates to the baseline, the additionality and the quantification of emission reductions.

For an asset owner this is a material risk that flat revenue models routinely miss: at renewal the counterfactual is rebuilt against a world that has moved on — cheaper technology, tighter regulation, a more ambitious benchmark. Credit volumes can legitimately fall at renewal even when the plant performs exactly as designed.

5

Crediting periods, permanence and reversals

Para. 31 quietly does the energy-versus-forestry work that Session 10 will pick up.

Activity typeCrediting period (para. 31(f))
Standard activities5 years, renewable twice (15 total) — or 10 years with no renewal
Activities involving removals15 years, renewable twice (45 total), subject to Supervisory Body approval
BothHost Party may specify a shorter period; no crediting period may start before 2021

Removals get up to three times the crediting horizon, because a forest or a geological store must be watched for far longer than a turbine needs to be paid for. The same asymmetry appears in the obligations: para. 31(d)(ii) requires activities to minimise the risk of non-permanence over multiple NDC implementation periods and, where reversals occur, ensure these are addressed in full; para. 50 requires participants to monitor potential reversals beyond the reduction itself.

“Addressed in full” is an open-ended liability attached to a finite revenue stream.

An avoided tonne from a wind farm is done. A stored tonne in a forest is a promise that must survive fire, harvest and politics — and if it fails, the rulebook says it must be made good in full. That single clause is why forestry credits price lower than energy credits of identical vintage, and why buffer pools exist at all.

Flag closed — the levies, exactly as drafted

Session 03 marked the 5% and 2% figures [?] and declined to assert them. Here they are from the decision text, with one detail almost everyone gets wrong.

Confirmed — and the 2% is a minimum, not a fixed rate

Para. 58: at issuance the registry administrator effects a first transfer of 5 per cent of the issued A6.4ERs to an account held by the Adaptation Fund, for developing country Parties particularly vulnerable to climate change. Para. 67(a) states it as “a levy of 5 per cent of A6.4ERs at issuance”; para. 66 routes it to the Adaptation Fund under decisions 13/CMA.1 and 1/CMP.14.

Para. 59: at issuance the administrator also effects a first transfer, for cancellation, of “a minimum of 2 per cent” of the issued A6.4ERs to the OMGE cancellation account. Para. 69(b) confirms the cancelled units “shall not be further transferred or used for any purpose”.

So my Session 03 note was right on the numbers and imprecise on the second: 2% is a floor. Decision paras. 8 and 9 also require the Supervisory Body to evaluate both the share of proceeds and the OMGE delivery — including the percentage applied — no later than 2026 and every five years after. That review is live now. [?] — whether it has concluded or changed the rate is beyond what I can verify here.

The CER carve-out — and why it is strange

Para. 75 lets CERs be used toward an NDC only if all of: the CDM activity was registered on or after 1 January 2013 (75(a)); the CERs are held in the mechanism registry and identified as pre-2021 reductions (75(b)); and they are used toward the first NDC only (75(c)). Temporary and long-term CERs “shall not be used towards NDCs” at all (75(f)).

Paragraph 75(d) then departs from the pattern above: for these CERs the host Party is “not required to apply a corresponding adjustment” and they are not subject to the share of proceeds.

Read against Fig. 4.3, the treatment differs in two respects. A new 6.4 unit costs the host 100 tonnes of headroom plus a 5% levy. A transitioned pre-2021 CER costs it nothing on either count — a deliberate Kyoto-era carve-over, limited to the first NDC, so the legacy pipeline could clear without hosts being retroactively charged for units issued under different rules.

What a host Party must have in place

Para. 26 lists the entry conditions. Read it as a checklist against Indonesia and the institutional picture from Session 03 sharpens considerably.

Para. 26 requirementIndonesia's corresponding provision
(a) Is a Party to the Paris AgreementYes
(b) Has prepared, communicated and maintains an NDCYes — and Perpres 110 Pasal 6, p.9 makes Alokasi Karbon the basis for setting it
(c) Has designated a national authority for the mechanism and told the secretariatPerpres 110 vests Otorisasi in the Menteri on recommendation of the sectoral minister — Pasal 68(2), p.48
(d) Has publicly indicated how participation contributes to sustainable development — expressly a national prerogativePasal 65(3), p.46 requires DRAM/DPP to contain a sustainable development impact analysis and a public consultation role
(e) Has publicly indicated the types of activity it would approve and how they contribute to its NDCPasal 58(3)(a), p.43 — the national carbon trading roadmap, coordinated by the steering committee

Para. 27 then permits a host Party to specify, in advance, the baseline approaches, methodological requirements including additionality, and crediting periods it will apply to activities it hosts. That is a substantial sovereign power and it maps directly onto Perpres 110's repeated delegation of methodology detail to Peraturan Menteri Terkait. Indonesia has the treaty room to set its own stricter methodology terms — the question is whether the implementing regulations use it. An explicit thing to look for in Session 08.

Indonesia did not invent a parallel system. It built a domestic mirror of the Article 6.4 cycle and kept the authorization gate in the same place.

The one real divergence is at issuance: PACM issues centrally, while Indonesia issues SPE-GRK ministerially and lets international standards issue non-SPE-GRK — but only with sectoral approval. Even on the international route, the state keeps a hand on the valve.

8

Three lenses

Government

Para. 27 lets you set baselines, additionality requirements and crediting periods in advance; para. 37 lets you commission standardized baselines. Both are unused leverage. And note the trap from §3: every new sectoral regulation shrinks your creditable pipeline — plan carbon policy and sectoral regulation as one thing.

Project developer

Additionality now has a written definition and it is stricter than the folk version — regulatory surplus is the test most projects fail. Check what law already requires before you build the counterfactual, not after. And budget for renewal being a genuine re-test of baseline and additionality.

Project / asset owner

Three numbers most models get wrong: you receive ≤93% of issued units, not 100%; the crediting period is 5+5+5 or 10, not the asset life; and volumes can legitimately fall at renewal. Model the waterfall in Fig. 4.3 explicitly.

Comprehension check

  1. Para. 33 requires baselines to be “below business as usual”. Why is that a fundamental break from the CDM rather than a tightening of it?
  2. Indonesia issues a regulation mandating high-efficiency motors in industry. What happens to the additionality of a motor-efficiency crediting project — and why is that a policy dilemma rather than a drafting error?
  3. A host country authorises 100,000 A6.4ERs. How many tonnes of NDC headroom does it give up, how many does it get paid for, and which paragraph creates that gap?
  4. Why do removal activities get a 15-year crediting period when other activities get 5 or 10 — and what obligation is the extra time paying for?
  5. A transitioned pre-2021 CER and a newly issued A6.4ER both represent one tonne. Name two ways the rulebook treats them differently, and say who benefits from each difference.
