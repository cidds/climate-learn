The Voluntary Market

A market with no regulator, no obligation, and until recently no sovereign in the loop. That freedom is why it grew fast, why it broke in 2023, and why Article 6 has now split it in two. This session closes the global block — and the last section is the one that matters most for Indonesia.

Demand without obligation

Session 05 defined a compliance instrument by legal obligation. The VCM is what you get when you remove exactly that and change nothing else.

Nobody is required to buy a voluntary credit. Demand comes from corporate net-zero commitments, product-level claims, supply-chain pressure, investor expectations and reputation. Which means the entire market rests on the buyer continuing to want to — a demand curve that can be revised by a press cycle.

Compliance marketVoluntary market

Speed to establishYears — legislation, MRV, registry, regulatorMonths
Project typesNarrow — what the regulator recognisesBroad — cookstoves, forests, soil, biochar, direct air capture
Chief vulnerabilityA loose cap kills the priceA credibility shock kills the demand

The VCM's great virtue and its great weakness are the same property.

No sovereign in the loop means it can finance a cookstove programme in a country with no carbon law at all, years before any compliance system could reach it. It also means nobody with public accountability was ever checking the arithmetic.

The main standards, and where to look them up

The largest are Verra (the Verified Carbon Standard), Gold Standard, the American Carbon Registry, the Climate Action Reserve, Plan Vivo, and ART for jurisdictional-scale forest programmes. Each runs its own registry, approves its own methodologies, and accredits its own auditors.4

For project-level and issuance data across all of them, the Berkeley Carbon Trading Project's Voluntary Registry Offsets Database is the standard research-grade aggregation — a university source rather than a market participant, which matters when the market participants are the ones being assessed.

3

What actually broke in 2023

The crisis is usually described in headlines. The underlying finding is more specific, and more useful, than the headlines were.

The most-cited peer-reviewed result is West et al. (2023) in Science, which examined REDD+ project sites and compared claimed avoided deforestation against independent counterfactual estimates.1

The evaluated projects claimed an aggregate 10.7 times more avoided deforestation than independent estimates justified. The discrepancy was not driven by the choice of forest-cover data, but by selection bias in the projects' control areas and in modelling approaches. The study covered 26 REDD+ project sites, of which 18 had sufficient public information on baselines.1

Read the mechanism, not the number: the failure was in the counterfactual, exactly where Session 00 said it would be.

Nothing was miscounted. The forests were measured correctly. What was wrong was the comparison — the estimate of what would have happened otherwise. Baseline construction is where the discretion lives, so it is where the error concentrates. This is the same point the Article 6.4 rulebook is built around when it demands baselines be “conservative… below business as usual” and confines them to three approved approaches (Session 04, ¶33 and ¶36).

Being fair to the evidence — the picture is now more nuanced than 2023

The 2023 finding was contested by the standards concerned, and the literature has since moved. A subsequent Science paper is titled “Tropical forest carbon offsets deliver partial gains amid persistent over-crediting” — a framing that concedes real gains alongside persistent over-issuance rather than dismissing the category outright.2 [?] — that paper is paywalled and I could not open it, so I rely only on its title and assert none of its specific findings.

The honest summary for your purposes: the direction of the finding is robust and repeatedly replicated — avoided-deforestation crediting has systematically over-issued — while the magnitude is contested and methodology-dependent. Anyone who tells you either “offsets are worthless” or “the critiques were debunked” is overstating what the evidence supports.

The integrity response — two bodies, two sides

The market's answer was to build governance it had deliberately done without. Note the structure: one body polices what is sold, another polices what is said about it.

Supply side — ICVCM and the Core Carbon Principles

The Integrity Council for the Voluntary Carbon Market describes itself as “an independent, non-profit governance body for the VCM, which aims to maximise its potential to finance climate solutions.” Its assessment framework evaluates both crediting programmes and categories of credits, and approved categories may carry the CCP label.3

Read principles 5, 7 and 10 against Session 04 and the convergence is unmistakable — additionality as counterfactual, conservative quantification, and explicit lock-in avoidance are the same three tests the Article 6.4 rulebook imposes at ¶¶33, 36 and 38. The private and treaty systems are converging on one quality standard, from opposite directions.

Demand side — VCMI and what a company may say

The Voluntary Carbon Markets Integrity Initiative governs the claim rather than the credit. Its Claims Code of Practice recognises three tiers — “Silver, Gold, and Platinum Carbon Integrity Claims recognize increasing levels of climate action” — each requiring “the purchase and retirement of high-quality carbon credits proportionate to its remaining emissions once a company has met, or demonstrated progress towards meeting, its near-term emission reduction targets.”5

Before any claim, foundational criteria apply: emissions accounting using national inventory methodologies or the GHG Protocol; near-term targets consistent with net zero by 2050 set through a credible science-aligned framework; and credits sourced “from outside the value chain of the company”.

The load-bearing sequencing: credits supplement internal reductions, they do not substitute for them. A company must set science-based near-term targets before using credits against residual emissions. This is Session 05's mitigation hierarchy, transplanted from compliance law into a voluntary code.

The irony worth noticing

The remedy for a voluntary market's integrity problem was more voluntary institutions. ICVCM and VCMI have no legal authority; a developer may ignore the CCP label and a company may ignore the Claims Code. They work only insofar as buyers demand them.

That is not necessarily a weakness — a label that buyers actually price is a real constraint. But it does mean the VCM's integrity architecture rests on the same foundation as its demand: continued voluntary participation. Which brings us to the one development that put a sovereign into the loop after all.

Article 6 split the VCM in two

This is the most commercially important thing in the session. The same project, the same tonne, can now produce two legally different assets — and which one you hold is decided by a ministry.

6

What can a buyer actually claim?

The question the bifurcation forces, and the one most corporate communications still get wrong.

Follow the accounting. A German company buys an unauthorised credit from an Indonesian project. No corresponding adjustment is applied. Under Perpres 110 Pasal 56(2) the Minister still counts that reduction toward Indonesia's NDC, because no CA has been issued.6 So the tonne is counted once — by Indonesia.

The German company's money paid for a reduction that counts toward Indonesia's target, not Germany's.

That is not a scandal and not a scam. It is climate finance — a private transfer that helped a developing country meet its own commitment. It is a genuinely good thing. What it is not is the neutralisation of the buyer's own emissions, and describing it that way is a category error rather than a marketing flourish.

Claim typeWhat it assertsWhich credit supports it
Offsetting“This tonne of mine is cancelled out by that tonne over there”Authorised, with a corresponding adjustment — otherwise the host is also counting it
Contribution“I financed a tonne of mitigation somewhere in the world”Either. Always defensible, because it claims only what actually happened

The market is moving toward contribution-style language for unauthorised credits — and it is the honest framing. It is also, for a host country, the more attractive one, for the reason in the next section.

7

Indonesia's quiet advantage

Put Pasal 68(1)(b) and Pasal 56(2) together and Indonesia has legislated something rather clever.

Sell the credit, keep the tonne

Pasal 68(1) huruf b permits cross-border offset trading without Otorisasi or a corresponding adjustment, provided the units are not used for any NDC or other international obligation. Pasal 56(2) then keeps those reductions inside Indonesia's own NDC accounting.6

The combined effect: Indonesia can host voluntary-market projects, let foreign money finance them, permit the credits to be sold abroad — and still count every tonne toward its own NDC. Foreign capital, domestic target progress, no headroom surrendered.

The price is that those credits are the lower-value tier and support only a contribution claim. So the policy question is a genuine trade, not a free lunch: authorise and earn more per tonne while losing headroom, or withhold authorisation and earn less while keeping the tonne. Perpres 110 gives the Minister that dial, per transaction, on sectoral recommendation.

Why forestry dominates Indonesian voluntary issuance — and energy does not

Three reinforcing reasons, all of which have now been established from primary text rather than assumed:

1 — Instrument fit. Session 01's table: forestry produces removals with reversal risk, dispersed counterparties and land-rights questions. That suits project-based voluntary crediting and results-based payment far better than an allowance market. Energy produces metered avoidance at a handful of large regulated installations — which is what an ETS is built for.

2 — Where the obligation already sits. Indonesian power and industry are heading into Perdagangan Emisi GRK under Pasal 62, with caps set from Alokasi Karbon. Once an installation is regulated, its abatement is largely inside the compliance system, and Session 04's ¶38 regulatory-surplus test bites: mitigation required by law cannot be credited.

3 — Buyer preference. Voluntary buyers have historically favoured nature-based credits for their story and co-benefits. Which is also why the integrity crisis landed hardest exactly there.

Session 10 tests all three against what Indonesia has actually issued.

8

Three lenses

Government

You hold a per-transaction dial, not a policy switch. Authorise and earn more per tonne but surrender headroom; withhold and earn less but keep the tonne. The right answer differs by sector and by how tight your NDC is — which makes it a portfolio decision that ought to be governed by the trading roadmap under Pasal 58(3)(a), not case by case.

Project developer

The VCM is the only route open to you if you sit outside a regulated installation and no sectoral offset limit has been set. Expect CCP-aligned methodology scrutiny regardless of standard — the private and treaty rulebooks have converged. And settle the authorisation question before you sign an offtake: it determines which tier you are selling into.

Project / asset owner

“Verified voluntary credit” names two assets at different prices. Before underwriting, ask three things: which standard, is it CCP-labelled, and is it authorised? A model priced on authorised-tier assumptions with an unauthorised asset is out by the whole spread — and for avoided-deforestation vintages, also carries the over-crediting risk the literature has repeatedly found.

Comprehension check — and the end of the global block

These five draw on all six sessions. If they come easily, the foundations are laid and Indonesia is next.

  1. Every party in the VCM issuance chain earns more when more credits are issued, except one. Name the exception, and explain why that party is least able to police quality.
  2. West et al. found roughly 10.7× over-claiming. Where in the credit-making machine did the error sit — and which two paragraphs of the Article 6.4 rulebook are designed to prevent exactly that?
  3. A company buys an unauthorised Indonesian credit and says it has “offset” its emissions. Explain precisely what is wrong, and what it could honestly say instead.
  4. Explain how Indonesia can sell a voluntary credit abroad and still count the tonne toward its own NDC. Cite the two articles that make it work.
  5. ICVCM Principles 5, 7 and 10 map onto three requirements in the Article 6.4 rulebook. Name them — and say what it means that a private body and a UN treaty converged on the same tests.
