Session 07 · Indonesia Carbon Economic Mechanisms

Indonesia's Climate Stack

Perpres 110 does not float free — it implements something. This session reads the document it implements: Indonesia's Second NDC, submitted 27 October 2025, which I fetched and worked through directly. It changed the shape of Indonesia's target, and its own numbers contain a fact about energy and forestry that should reframe how you read everything after this.

The stack — NEK is one floor, not the building

Carbon pricing sits near the bottom of a tall structure. Knowing which floor you are on prevents the commonest error: treating Perpres 110 as Indonesia's climate policy rather than as one of its instruments.

Fig. 7.1 — All instruments named are cited in the Second NDC itself, pp. 4, 6, 10.1

!

Flag closed — the figures were right, and are now superseded

Session 02 marked Indonesia's 31.89% / 43.20% targets [?] and refused to rely on them. That caution paid off twice over.

Verified — and overtaken

The Second NDC confirms the history directly: the First NDC pledged 29% unconditional / 41% conditional against the 2030 business-as-usual scenario, up from a 2010 pledge of 26%; the Enhanced NDC (2022) raised this to 31.89% unconditional / 43.20% conditional against BAU in 2030.1 So the figures I flagged were correct — for the previous NDC.

The Second NDC replaces that architecture. It is no longer a percentage against a projected baseline. It is a level of emission relative to an actual reference year.

StructureUnconditional / conditional pairThree scenarios — CPOS, LCCP_L, LCCP_H

Indonesia moved from a BAU-relative target to a reference-year target — and that is exactly the shift Session 01 described.

A BAU-relative pledge is only as demanding as the baseline it is measured against, and the baseline is a projection the pledging country builds itself. A level-of-emission target against an observed year removes that discretion entirely. The NDC says so in its own footnote: the 2019 reference was chosen because it is “the most current methodologies and GHG inventory pre-COVID-19 to better inform Indonesia's actual state.”1

This is a genuine methodological strengthening, and it is also why every percentage figure you have previously heard about Indonesia's target is now the wrong currency. Do not mix them.

Under every scenario Indonesia submitted, 2035 emissions are above 2019.

Even the most ambitious — LCCP_L, 1.5°C-aligned and dependent on international support — lands 9.8% higher than the reference year. This is not a criticism smuggled in from outside; it is Indonesia's own submitted table. What the NDC promises is that emissions peak in 2030 and then turn, reaching net-negative by 2060 in both LCCP scenarios.

Hold this when you read the market design: a growing emissions envelope is the context in which the cap under Pasal 62 will be set.

Energy grows. Forests do the work.

Decompose the 2019→2035 path in the ambitious scenario and the strategy becomes unmistakable — and it directly answers your energy-versus-forestry question, from Indonesia's own numbers.

Indonesia's climate target is a forestry target with an energy problem attached.

That is the single most useful sentence I can give you for the sessions ahead, and it comes straight from the submitted table. FOLU Net Sink 2030 is not a side programme — it is the mechanism by which the national number works at all. Energy is planned to nearly double its emissions while the forest sector absorbs the difference.

Three consequences to carry into Sessions 08–11

1 — The carbon market and the NDC pull in different directions in forestry. If FOLU must deliver a 390,000 Gg swing to make the national target work, every forest tonne authorised for export under Pasal 68 is a tonne taken out of that swing. This is the corresponding-adjustment trade from Session 03, but now with a magnitude attached: Indonesia has very little forest headroom to sell.

2 — Energy is where crediting room exists, and where the cap will bind. Energy emissions are planned to grow, which means the Batas Atas Emisi under Pasal 62 will be set against a rising envelope. That makes an intensity-benchmarked design far more likely than an absolute one at installation level — and reopens exactly the translation problem flagged in Session 01.

3 — The 2019 composition explains the emphasis. The NDC reports 2019 emissions dominated by LUCF including peat fires at 50.13%, energy at 34.49%. But the First BTR reports 2022 emissions of 1,383 Gg-scale dominated by energy at 69.01% with LUCF at 22.58%.1 The two years differ enormously because 2019 was a severe fire year. Indonesia's emissions profile swings on fire — which is why the reference-year choice matters so much, and why permanence risk in Session 04 is not an abstraction here.

Read PP 33/2023 next to Session 04's additionality test

Minimum energy performance standards and mandatory energy management are exactly the kind of instrument that triggers the regulatory surplus bar in Decision -/CMA.3 ¶38: creditable mitigation must exceed what law or regulation already requires.

So the more thoroughly Indonesia mandates efficiency, the smaller the creditable energy-efficiency pipeline becomes — the dilemma named in Session 04, now with a specific regulation attached to it. For a developer this is a live diligence question: does your efficiency project go beyond the applicable MEPS, or merely meet it? If it merely meets it, it is compliance, not a credit.

Marine and blue carbon — a NEK implementing regulation already exists

Worth flagging because it shows the implementing layer is being built sector by sector: the NDC cites Minister of Marine Affairs and Fisheries Regulation No. 1 of 2025 on the Governance of Carbon Economic Value Implementation in the Marine Sector, alongside KKP Decree No. 52/2024 on the climate change mitigation roadmap for the marine and fisheries sector.1

That is a Peraturan Menteri Terkait of exactly the kind Perpres 110 repeatedly delegates to. When Session 08 asks where additionality and methodology detail actually live, this is the shape of the answer — and it means the answer will differ by sector.

5

Where NEK sits — the NDC names Perpres 110 directly

You do not have to infer the relationship. The NDC states it.

“The Government of Indonesia has promulgated Presidential Regulation No. 110 of 2025 concerning the implementation of carbon pricing and national greenhouse gas emissions management. The Regulation serves as a legal framework to implement NDC towards low carbon and climate resilience. It also prescribes carbon pricing, including arrangements for carbon trading, carbon levies and result based payments and national registry system. Sectoral Ministries and Agencies as the responsible institutions on the achievement of NDC's target will fully engage in the implementation…”1

Three things this settles. First, Perpres 110 is subordinate to the NDC — it is the delivery instrument, not the objective. Second, the instrument list in the NDC matches Pasal 55(2) exactly: trading, levy, results-based payment, registry. Third, sectoral ministries are named as the responsible institutions, which is precisely why Perpres 110 hands so much to Menteri Terkait — the offset limit, the ETS design, the methodology detail.

The NDC also names PP No. 26 of 2025 on Environmental Protection and Management Planning, which sets the framework for the national RPPLH and elaborates decarbonisation, resilience and disaster risk reduction as concrete policy.

6

The national focal point has moved

A short but important institutional note, and a preview of Session 09.

From KLHK's DG Climate Change to the Ministry of Environment

The NDC records that the Directorate General of Climate Change, established under Presidential Regulation No. 16 of 2015 within the Ministry of Environment and Forestry, “serves as the National Focal Point for the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change” — and then adds, plainly: “Under new government, this mandate has been handed over to Ministry of Environment.”1

This confirms from primary text what Session 00 left carefully unstated: the former KLHK has been separated, and the UNFCCC focal-point mandate now sits with the Ministry of Environment — which is also the Menteri to whom Perpres 110 assigns Otorisasi (Pasal 68(2)) and SPE-GRK issuance (Pasal 65(5)).

The NDC names the coordination set explicitly: Ministry of Environment, BAPPENAS and Ministry of Finance for climate finance and international negotiation, with line ministries — Forestry, Energy and Mineral Resources, Industry, Agriculture, Marine Affairs and Fisheries — and Ministry of Home Affairs for subnational implementation. Session 09 maps this against Perpres 110's steering committee.

7

Three lenses

Government

Two of your three scenarios depend on international support arriving. That makes Article 6 revenue and results-based payment part of the target's feasibility, not a bonus. But the FOLU arithmetic means forest tonnes are the ones you can least afford to authorise for export — so the money you need and the asset you can sell are not the same asset.

Project developer

In energy, check your project against PP 33/2023 MEPS and PP 40/2025 targets before building an additionality case — if the regulation already requires it, it is not creditable. In forestry, expect authorisation to be hard to obtain: those tonnes are load-bearing for the national target.

Project / asset owner

The NDC is now stated in absolute tonnes against a 2019 reference, so any diligence memo quoting “31.89% below BAU” is working from a superseded framework. Ask which NDC the counterparty's model was built against — and note that energy sector emissions are planned to rise, which shapes how tight any cap can realistically be.

Comprehension check

  1. Why is a “level of emission relative to 2019” target methodologically stronger than “31.89% below BAU in 2030”? Name the discretion it removes.
  2. Under LCCP_L, 2035 emissions are 9.8% above 2019 — yet this is the 1.5°C-aligned scenario. Explain how both statements are consistent.
  3. FOLU swings 389,796 Gg from source to sink. Using Session 03, explain why that number makes forest carbon exports strategically expensive for Indonesia.
  4. An industrial client proposes an energy-efficiency crediting project. Which two regulations do you check first, and what are you checking for?
  5. Indonesia's 2019 emissions were 50% LUCF; its 2022 emissions were 69% energy. What single factor explains most of that swing, and what does it imply for permanence risk?
