One picture before the words

Almost every term below sits somewhere on one of two production lines. Both lines end in the same place — a tradable Unit Karbon — but they are built in opposite directions, and knowing which line a term belongs to resolves most of the ambiguity you have been hearing.

A

The traded unit

Start here, because this is where the 30%-understanding usually breaks. “Carbon credit” is used in casual conversation for four different things that are legally distinct.

Allowance

Kuota Emisi GRK Pasal 1(15) · p.4

A permission to emit, created by government when it divides a cap into shares. It is not proof that anything was reduced — it is a licence to release a set tonnage.

“jumlah Emisi GRK yang dapat dilepaskan ke atmosfer oleh Instalasi yang Diatur” — the amount of GHG an obligated installation may release.

Why this matters for a power plant

An allowance is scarce only because the state made it scarce. Its price is a policy output, not a market discovery — which is why an ETS cap that is set too loosely produces a near-zero price and no behaviour change. For a regulated installation (Instalasi yang Diatur, Pasal 1(16), p.4), allowances are a balance-sheet liability the moment the compliance period opens.

Credit

Unit Karbon Pasal 1(18) · p.4

The umbrella tradable instrument. Critically, Indonesian law defines it broadly enough to include allowances — so in Indonesia the allowance is a species of Unit Karbon, not its opposite.

“hasil pengurangan dan/atau penyerapan emisi yang disertifikatkan melalui skema sertifikasi domestik, sertifikasi internasional, atau Kuota Emisi GRK” — certified reductions or removals under a domestic scheme, an international scheme, or an emission quota.

The trap this creates

In EU or global discussion, “allowance vs credit” is a hard binary. In Perpres 110 they are nested. So when an Indonesian document says Perdagangan Unit Karbon, you cannot tell from the phrase alone whether ETS allowances, domestic offsets, or Verra-issued credits are in scope — you have to read what certification path is named. Watch for this in every Indonesian source from here on.

Emission reduction certificate

SPE-GRK Pasal 1(37) · p.6

Indonesia's domestic credit: proof of a reduction that has passed MRV and been recorded in the unit registry with a registry number.

“bukti pengurangan emisi … yang telah melalui MRV, serta tercatat dalam SRUK dalam bentuk nomor dan/atau kode registri.”

Why “registry number” is in the definition

A credit that is not uniquely serialised in a registry is not a credit — it is a claim. Serialisation is what makes double-selling detectable. Note that Perpres 110/2025 points SPE-GRK at SRUK, a unit-level registry it introduces separately from SRN PPI (see cluster E) — a structural change from the previous regime.

Offset

Pengimbangan / Offset Emisi GRK Pasal 1(24) · p.5

Not a thing but a use: reducing emissions in one place to compensate for emissions made somewhere else. Any credit becomes “an offset” at the moment it is retired against someone's footprint.

Say it precisely

“I bought an offset” is loose. Precise: “I bought a credit and retired it as an offset against my scope-1 emissions.” Three separate events — purchase, retirement, claim — and integrity failures happen at each one independently.

B

The number behind the unit

Every credit is a subtraction: what would have happened, minus what did happen. Both halves are constructed, and that construction is where value and controversy both come from.

Baseline (business-as-usual)

Baseline Emisi GRK Pasal 1(9) · p.3

The counterfactual: projected emissions “tanpa intervensi kebijakan dan/atau teknologi mitigasi” — without policy or mitigation-technology intervention.

Why the baseline drives credit volume

Credits issued = baseline − actual. Raise the baseline and you manufacture credits without touching a single tonne of real emissions. This is why baseline-setting is procedurally defended (methodology approval, third-party validation, regulator sign-off) rather than left to the developer — and why the 2023–24 VCM integrity crisis was largely a baseline crisis. Perpres 110 sets baselines at national, sector and provincial scope (Pasal 15, p.15).

Additionality

— not defined in Pasal 1 [?] global concept

The test that the reduction would not have happened anyway — without carbon revenue, the project is not financially, technologically or legally viable.

Notable: across all 44 defined terms in Perpres 110/2025 Pasal 1 (pp. 2–7), additionality is not among them.1 The concept is expected to live in ministerial-level methodology rules rather than the Presidential Regulation. [?] — I have verified the absence from Pasal 1 directly; where exactly it is operationalised is to be confirmed in Session 08 against the implementing regulations.

MRV

Pengukuran, Pelaporan, dan Verifikasi Pasal 1(26) · p.5

The assurance chain that makes a tonne credible: measure it, report it, have an independent party verify it. Perpres 110 defines MRV as covering both mitigation and adaptation actions.

Where the cost sits

MRV is the dominant recurring transaction cost of a crediting project, and it scales poorly for small projects — the reason programme/aggregated approaches exist. For an energy-efficiency retrofit, metering design decided at engineering stage determines whether MRV is cheap or ruinous later. Decide it early.

Activity data × emission factor

Data Aktivitas · Faktor Emisi GRK Pasal 1(35),(36) · p.6

The arithmetic of every inventory: how much you did (MWh generated, tonnes of clinker) × emissions per unit of doing it. Perpres 110 requires IPCC guidelines for the calculation (Pasal 13(5), p.15).

Removals / sinks

Serapan GRK Pasal 1(34) · p.6

GHG absorbed out of the atmosphere, naturally or by technology — as opposed to emissions avoided. The distinction drives the whole energy-vs-forestry contrast we take up in Session 10.

C

System archetypes

The “compliance vs market”, “absolute vs intensity” and “ETS vs beyond-ETS” threads you heard all resolve into a small set of design choices. Here are the labels; Session 01 does the economics.

Compliance vs voluntary

global framing

Compliance: the state obliges you to hold and surrender units; non-compliance is a legal breach. Voluntary (VCM): you buy because you chose to — a reputational or contractual commitment, not a legal one. Same-looking credit, entirely different demand driver and price.

Cap-and-trade (ETS)

Perdagangan Emisi GRK Pasal 1(23) · p.5

Government fixes the quantity and lets the market discover the price. Perpres 110 defines it narrowly as the transaction of Kuota Emisi GRK between business actors — i.e. allowance trading specifically.

Carbon trading (umbrella)

Perdagangan Karbon Pasal 1(22) · p.5

The wider category: any market-based buying and selling of Unit Karbon. Perdagangan Karbon ⊃ Perdagangan Emisi GRK. Two similar Indonesian phrases, one nested inside the other — reading them as synonyms will mislead you.

Carbon tax / levy

Pungutan Atas Karbon Pasal 1(30) · p.6

Government fixes the price and lets the market determine the quantity — the mirror image of cap-and-trade. Perpres 110's definition is notably wide: a state levy, central or regional, on goods, services or activities with carbon content or carbon-emitting potential.

Beyond-ETS compliance instruments

This is one answer to “compliance but beyond ETS”. The compliance toolkit also includes performance standards, mandatory intensity benchmarks, and results-based payment — the point being that an ETS is one instrument in a family, not the whole of compliance. Session 05 maps the full family.

Results-based payment

Pembayaran Berbasis Kinerja Pasal 1(25) · p.5

Payment for verified outcomes without necessarily creating a tradable unit — the mechanism behind most REDD+ and donor climate finance. Perpres 110 explicitly extends it to validated non-carbon benefits, not just tonnes.

Absolute vs intensity target

global framing

Absolute: a hard tonnage ceiling — emissions must fall in reality. Intensity: emissions per unit of output (tCO₂e/MWh, per tonne of product) — a plant can grow output and still comply while total emissions rise. Indonesia's power-sector ETS design has used intensity benchmarking. [?] — widely reported, but to be verified against the implementing ministerial regulation in Session 08 rather than asserted now.

Why a government might choose intensity anyway

Intensity targets are politically survivable in a fast-growing economy because they do not cap growth — they reward efficiency per unit. The trade-off is that they are not directly reconcilable with an absolute NDC number, so a government running intensity benchmarks domestically still has to translate results into absolute tonnes for its NDC accounting. Perpres 110's Alokasi Karbon (Pasal 1(2), p.3) is defined in absolute CO₂e terms, which makes that translation an explicit design requirement rather than an afterthought.

D

Accounting & integrity

This cluster is the entire subject matter of Paris Article 6, and Perpres 110 imports its vocabulary directly — including one term left in English inside the Indonesian legal text.

NDC

Kontribusi yang Ditetapkan secara Nasional Pasal 1(1) · p.2

A country's self-set, periodically renewed climate commitment under the Paris Agreement. It is the accounting boundary against which every transfer below is judged.

Corresponding adjustment

Corresponding Adjustment (kept in English in the Indonesian text) Pasal 1(28) · p.5

When a unit leaves the country, the seller must add that tonne back to its own emissions books, so the buyer and seller do not both count it.

“penyesuaian akuntansi Unit Karbon di NDC untuk menghindari terjadinya pencatatan ganda setelah pemindahan Unit Karbon ke luar negeri.”

Why this is the hinge of the whole architecture

A corresponding adjustment makes an exported credit expensive to the host country in NDC terms — it sells a tonne and simultaneously makes its own target harder. That single fact drives host-country export restrictions, authorization gatekeeping, and the price gap between adjusted and unadjusted credits. Every Article 6 negotiation runs through it. Authorization

Otorisasi Pasal 1(27) · p.5

Ministerial approval to use a Unit Karbon toward another country's NDC, an international mitigation obligation, or other purposes. In effect, an export licence for carbon.

Developer and owner implication

Without authorization your credit cannot legitimately be sold to an Article 6 or CORSIA-type compliance buyer — it is domestic-use-only. Authorization status is therefore a primary valuation variable, not paperwork: two physically identical credits from the same project trade at materially different prices depending on it. For an asset owner, whether authorization is obtainable is a diligence question to answer before financial close, not after.

Reserve carbon

Karbon Cadangan Pasal 1(17) · p.4

Carbon set aside at national level as a risk buffer for meeting the NDC. A distinctly Indonesian design feature: the state holds back headroom rather than allocating everything out.

Carbon allocation

Alokasi Karbon Pasal 1(2) · p.3

The total CO₂e permitted nationally over a period, “sesuai kapasitas nasional”. It is set jointly by the forestry, environment, energy, industry, agriculture, finance and planning ministries (Pasal 4(1), p.8) and becomes the basis for the NDC itself (Pasal 6, p.9).

ITMO — the term worth slowing down for

— no Indonesian legal equivalent in Pasal 1 Paris Art. 6.2

Internationally Transferred Mitigation Outcome. Read the four words literally, because each one was fought over and each one carries meaning.

WordWhat it rules in — and out
InternationallyIt only becomes an ITMO when it crosses a border. A reduction traded between two Indonesian companies is never an ITMO, however it was certified.
TransferredOwnership moves from one country's account to another's. The moment of “first transfer” is what triggers the seller's corresponding adjustment.
MitigationIt must be real mitigation counted against a NDC — not finance, not technology transfer, not a promise.
OutcomeThe load-bearing word. Deliberately not “credit”, “unit”, or “certificate” — because an ITMO need not come from a project at all.

That last word is the answer to your curiosity. Negotiators chose “outcome” so that a country could transfer any mitigation result it can measure and account for — a sectoral policy result, a programme-wide reduction, a fleet-level improvement — not only a project-generated credit. CDM thinking was project-shaped; Article 6.2 was written to be bigger than projects.

ITMO vs A6.4ER — the distinction that actually matters in a deal

ITMO (Article 6.2) is the decentralised route. Two countries sign a bilateral agreement, define their own methodologies and quality rules between themselves, and transfer. No UN body issues the unit — the UN only receives the reporting. Fast, flexible, and only as rigorous as the two parties choose to be.

A6.4ER (Article 6.4) is the centralised route: a UN supervisory body approves the methodology, accredits the verifier, and issues the unit — the architecture PACM inherits from the CDM. Slower, more standardised, more portable.

The relationship: an A6.4ER becomes an ITMO when it is authorised and transferred across a border. So they are not rival units — one is a unit type, the other is a status a unit acquires on export. Session 03 does this properly.

Why Perpres 110 never uses the word

Indonesian law reaches the same place by a different road. Rather than importing “ITMO” as a defined object, Perpres 110 regulates the two acts that constitute one: Otorisasi (Pasal 1(27), p.5) — the state's permission to send a unit abroad for another country's NDC — and Corresponding Adjustment (Pasal 1(28), p.5) — the accounting entry that follows. An Indonesian Unit Karbon that has been authorised and transferred is an ITMO in Paris terms, even though no Indonesian statute calls it that.

Practical consequence: when you read Indonesian regulation you will not find the word, and when you read a bilateral term sheet you will find nothing else. Same object, two vocabularies — you now hold both.

What an ITMO deal looks like in practice

The recognisable pattern is a buyer government with a hard NDC gap and money, and a host country with abatement potential: Switzerland has signed bilateral Article 6.2 agreements with several host countries, and Japan's JCM — which Indonesia joined in 2013 — is the longest-running bilateral crediting relationship of this type. [?] — structure is well established; specific counterparties, volumes and current Article 6.2 alignment status to be cited to primary agreements in Session 03 rather than asserted here.

For Indonesia the strategic question is not “can we sell ITMOs” but “should we” — since every authorised export makes the domestic NDC harder (see the corresponding-adjustment card above). That tension is the heart of Session 03 and returns in Session 11's government lens.

E

Plumbing & actors

Who records, who checks, who trades, who decides. These are the terms that turn a policy concept into an operating system.

National climate registry

SRN PPI Pasal 1(19) · p.4

The national system holding data on mitigation and adaptation actions and resources at NDC level. Think of it as the country's action ledger.

Carbon unit registry

SRUK Pasal 1(20) · p.4

A separate registry for information on Unit Karbon themselves. Perpres 110/2025 splits the action ledger (SRN PPI) from the unit ledger (SRUK) — a structural change worth watching, since SPE-GRK is defined as being recorded in SRUK, not SRN PPI.1

Carbon exchange

Bursa Karbon Pasal 1(29) · p.5

The system that records carbon trades and ownership status of units. In Indonesia this is operationalised as IDXCarbon, launched September 2023. [?] — launch date and operator widely reported; to be cited to OJK/IDX primary source in Session 09.

Validation & verification body

LVV — not defined in Pasal 1 [?] absent from this regulation

The accredited independent third party that checks a project design (validation) and later its claimed reductions (verification) — the role the CDM called a DOE.

Verified directly: LVV appears nowhere among the 44 defined terms of Perpres 110/2025 Pasal 1 (pp. 2–7), which reaches the function only through the “V” in MRV.1 Its accreditation regime therefore sits below Presidential-Regulation level — a ministerial regulation and national accreditation body. DRAM and DPP — the fork disguised as paperwork

Both are project design documents: they describe the project, prove it meets the applicable requirements, and set out in detail how much emission reduction or removal it will produce. They differ in one variable — whose rulebook you are writing to — and that single variable determines which market you end up in.

Get it wrong and you are not filling in a different form later — you are re-baselining and re-validating the project against a different rulebook.

How the three lenses see this differently

Government gets a sorting mechanism. By defining the fork in law, the state knows at design stage which pipeline a project is in, and therefore which units may later need Otorisasi and a corresponding adjustment. It is an early-warning system for NDC leakage, built into a definition.

Developer faces a real cost-and-access trade-off. The domestic DRAM route is cheaper and administratively closer, but its demand depends on how ambitious the domestic cap becomes. The international DPP route reaches deeper-pocketed buyers but imports a foreign standard's methodology burden, fees and timelines.

Project owner / investor should treat this as a valuation input, not a compliance detail. The document type constrains the exit routes for the credit stream, which constrains who can buy, which constrains price. Ask which document was filed before you underwrite the revenue line.

What this does not tell you yet

Pasal 1 defines the two documents but not their contents, approval steps, or whether a project can hold both. Those sit in implementing regulation — the same layer that holds additionality and LVV accreditation. Flagged as an open item for Session 08; I will not guess at the procedure before reading it.

Who “the Minister” means

Menteri · Menteri Terkait · Pelaku Usaha Pasal 1(42),(43),(44) · p.7

Menteri = the environment minister (the NEK coordinator). Menteri Terkait = the sectoral ministers for each NDC sector. Pelaku Usaha = the regulated business actor. Every obligation in the regulation attaches to one of these three, so identifying which one is named tells you who actually holds the power in any given article.

Sector & sub-sector

Sektor · Sub Sektor Pasal 1(38),(39) · p.7 · Pasal 9 · pp.11–12

Defined explicitly as NDC sectors, not administrative jurisdictions. Pasal 9 lists them: energy, waste, IPPU, agriculture, forestry, marine & fisheries — with sub-sectors including power generation and industry (your energy focus), alongside forestry, peat & mangrove, and blue carbon.

Two habits worth adopting

Name the instrument, not the category. “Carbon market” covers an ETS, a crediting mechanism and a voluntary standard, which behave differently. Saying which one removes most ambiguity before it starts.

Separate the unit from its use. A credit is an instrument; retiring it is an act; the claim made afterwards is a third thing. Most disputes about integrity are disputes about one of the three being described as another.

1

The missing cost

A coal plant's cost sheet is honest about coal, labour, capital, and maintenance. It is silent about the climate damage of the CO₂ leaving the stack — not because anyone is lying, but because no one sends an invoice for that damage. The cost is real; it simply lands on people who were not party to the transaction.

This is an externality, and it produces a specific, predictable failure: because the plant's private cost is lower than the social cost, the plant rationally emits more than is collectively optimal. Every actor behaves correctly and the aggregate outcome is still wrong.

The purpose of carbon pricing is not to punish emitters. It is to repair a bookkeeping error.

Put the missing cost onto the ledger, and the same profit-maximising behaviour that previously produced excess emissions starts producing abatement instead. Nothing about the firm's motivation has to change — only its cost function.

The two classical answers, and why both survive in today's architecture

Pigou (1920) argued the state should levy a tax equal to the marginal external damage, so the private cost rises to meet the social cost. That lineage runs directly to today's carbon tax and to Indonesia's Pungutan Atas Karbon.1

Coase (1960) argued that if property rights over the externality are clearly defined and bargaining is cheap, the parties will trade their way to the efficient outcome without the state setting a price at all — the state's job is to define and enforce the right. That lineage runs to cap-and-trade: an allowance is a property right in emissions.2

Neither displaced the other. Modern systems run both simultaneously, which is exactly why Perpres 110 defines a levy and an allowance market as parallel NEK instruments rather than alternatives.

Read the middle two columns as a pair and the whole architecture snaps into focus: a price instrument and a quantity instrument are the same trade made in opposite directions. One fixes price and lets emissions float; the other fixes emissions and lets price float. You cannot fix both — that is not a policy failing, it is arithmetic.

3

Why a price beats a mandate

This is the single argument that justifies the existence of carbon markets. If you take one thing from Session 01, take this.

Abatement does not cost the same everywhere. Replacing worn industrial motors can save money outright; retrofitting CCS onto an existing coal unit is among the most expensive things you can do. Between those extremes sits everything else.

Now suppose a government mandates that every plant cut emissions by the same 20%. It has just forced the expensive plant to buy costly abatement while cheap abatement at the plant next door goes undone. Same tonnes, far higher national bill.

A carbon market does not reduce emissions. The cap does that. The market reduces the cost of the reduction.

Set the price, and every firm abates up to the point where its own next tonne costs more than the price — and pays for the rest. Cheap abatement everywhere gets used before expensive abatement anywhere. That is the whole efficiency argument, and it is why the same tonnage is delivered for less money than any mandate could achieve.

The formal version — the equimarginal principle

Total abatement cost is minimised when the marginal abatement cost is equal across all sources. A uniform mandate equalises quantities abated, which guarantees unequal marginal costs and therefore guarantees waste. A price equalises marginal costs and lets quantities differ — which is the condition for the cheapest possible delivery of a given target.3

Tradable permits reach the same point by a different route: firms with high abatement costs buy allowances from firms with low ones, and trading continues until no further mutually profitable trade exists — which is precisely where marginal costs have equalised. Coase's insight, mechanised.

Why the negative-cost measures matter more than they look

Measures 1–3 pay for themselves and are still not done. That apparent paradox — money left on the table — is well documented in the energy-efficiency literature, and it shapes what a carbon price can and cannot achieve in this segment. The barriers are not price: they are split incentives (the landlord buys the chiller, the tenant pays the bill), capital rationing, information cost, and the absence of clear internal ownership.

Consequence for instrument design: a carbon price alone will not unlock negative-cost abatement, because those measures were already profitable and were skipped anyway. Efficiency needs the fourth lever — obligation, finance, or information — not the second or third. Any policy package that relies on price alone leaves the cheapest tonnes on the table.

Both instruments reach the efficient outcome if the government knows every firm's abatement costs. It never does. Once you admit that uncertainty, the two stop being equivalent — and the choice becomes principled rather than ideological.

Weitzman's rule — the actual decision criterion

Weitzman (1974) showed the choice turns on the relative slopes of the marginal damage and marginal abatement cost curves under uncertainty.4

If marginal damage is steep — one extra tonne does sharply more harm — getting the quantity wrong is costly, so use a quantity instrument. If marginal abatement cost is steep — a small overshoot in required abatement gets very expensive very fast — getting the price wrong is costly, so use a price instrument.

For CO₂ specifically the standard reading is that it is a stock pollutant: damage depends on cumulative atmospheric concentration, so the marginal damage of one particular year's tonne is comparatively flat. On Weitzman's criterion that argues for a price instrument. In practice governments overwhelmingly built quantity instruments anyway — because a cap is what you can put in a treaty pledge, and “tax” is what loses elections. The theory points one way and the politics the other, which is why hybrids won.

Absolute vs intensity — the fork you asked about

Same instrument, two ways to write the obligation. The difference is invisible in a policy summary and decisive in a growing economy.

Absolute: a hard tonnage. “Emit no more than X tonnes.” Intensity: emissions per unit of output. “Emit no more than X tonnes per MWh / per tonne of cement.” An intensity target rewards efficiency but places no ceiling on total emissions — because it scales with production.

6

Why no real system is a pure form

Every mature system bolts price features onto quantity instruments, because pure forms fail in predictable ways.

The three standard bolt-ons

Price floor — a reserve price at auction, or a top-up tax. Stops the price collapsing when the cap turns out to be loose or a recession cuts demand. Without one, an ETS can deliver its tonnage target and still send no investment signal at all.

Price ceiling / cost-containment reserve — extra allowances released if the price spikes. Protects industry from a cost shock, at the cost of loosening the cap in exactly the year it was binding.

Banking and borrowing — carry allowances forward (or draw them back). Smooths prices across years and lets firms time abatement investment sensibly, rather than optimising to an arbitrary compliance date.

Indonesia's version, and why it is not quite the same thing

Karbon Cadangan — reserve carbon — is defined as carbon allocated at national level for risk control in achieving the NDC target (Pasal 1(17), p.4).5 Perpres 110 also requires that Alokasi Karbon be set taking it into account (Pasal 3(3), p.8).

Note the stated purpose carefully: it is a buffer against the country missing its target, not primarily a market price-stabilisation tool. A cost-containment reserve protects firms from price spikes; Karbon Cadangan protects the state from NDC failure. They may end up being used similarly, but they are justified differently — and if the reserve is ever released into the market, which purpose governs will matter a great deal. An open question for Session 08.

7

“Compliance vs market priority”

You heard this phrase and it is genuinely ambiguous — it gets used for two different debates. Both are worth holding separately.

Reading A — the mitigation hierarchy

Should a regulated firm be allowed to buy its way out, or must it cut its own emissions first? Nearly every compliance system answers “cut first, offset the residual”, and enforces it with a quantitative limit on how much of an obligation may be met with offset credits rather than own-abatement or allowances.

The reasoning: unlimited offset use lets the cap be met on paper while the regulated sector never transforms — and since offsets are usually cheaper than deep sectoral abatement, an unlimited system would buy the cheapest tonnes forever and never build the capital stock the transition requires.

Reading B — which market a country builds first

A compliance market creates guaranteed demand, but needs legislation, an MRV system, a registry, and a regulator — years of work, and it makes enemies. A voluntary market can be stood up much faster, but its demand is discretionary, thin, and vulnerable to integrity shocks.

The trap in sequencing voluntary-first: projects get built against a demand curve that can evaporate, and the country establishes prices and expectations that the later compliance system inherits. Indonesia has been building both tracks in parallel — Perpres 110 defines domestic trading, international transfer, offsets, RBP and the levy as one instrument family rather than a sequence.5

Full treatment in Session 05 (compliance beyond ETS) and Session 06 (VCM). Flagged here only so the phrase stops being ambiguous when you next hear it.

8

The same choice, three lenses

Government

Choosing an instrument is choosing what you want certainty about — and what you can politically survive. A cap buys you a defensible NDC number; a levy buys you revenue and administrative simplicity. Ask of any proposal: what does this guarantee, and what has it left floating?

Project developer

Your revenue is a policy artifact, not a market discovery. Under a cap, demand for your credits exists because the state made it scarce; under a levy, it may not exist at all. Diligence the instrument that creates your buyer before you diligence the buyer.

Project / asset owner

Instrument type sets your revenue volatility profile. Cap-and-trade means a floating price you cannot hedge far out; a levy means a stable administered price with no upside. If your model uses a flat carbon price for fifteen years, it is describing a levy — check that is the instrument you are actually in.

Why a treaty at all

Session 01 ended with a government correcting an externality inside its own borders. That works for river pollution. It fails completely for CO₂, for one reason: the damage does not stay where the emission happened.

A tonne emitted in Java warms Europe exactly as much as a tonne emitted in Germany. So a country that acts alone bears 100% of the abatement cost and captures a vanishingly small share of the benefit. Every country faces that arithmetic, and the rational individual response is to wait for someone else to move. This is the free-rider problem, and it is why domestic instruments alone cannot solve climate change no matter how well designed.

The treaty layer exists to make unilateral action rational — by making it reciprocal.

Everything above the domestic instrument — pledges, transparency rules, review cycles, market mechanisms — is machinery for one purpose: giving each country enough confidence that others are also moving to justify moving itself. Read the whole architecture as a solution to a trust problem and it stops looking bureaucratic.

2

Three legal instruments, one stack

They are not replacements for one another. All three remain legally in existence; each nests inside the one before it.

UNFCCC — the framework adopted 1992 · in force 1994

The UNFCCC is a framework convention: it sets the objective and the institutions but almost no obligations. Its Article 2 objective is stabilising greenhouse gas concentrations at a level that prevents dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system.1

Two things it created that still govern everything: the principle of CBDR-RC, and the Annex I / non-Annex I split between industrialised and developing countries. That split is the hinge the next thirty years turns on.

Kyoto Protocol — binding targets for some adopted 1997 · in force 2005

Kyoto took the Annex I / non-Annex I split and made it operative: binding emission targets for Annex I countries only. Developing countries, Indonesia among them, took on no target.

It also introduced market mechanisms into international law for the first time — three of them: JI (Art. 6), the CDM (Art. 12), and international emissions trading (Art. 17).2 The CDM is the ancestor of everything in Session 04.

Paris Agreement — universal and self-determined adopted 2015 · in force 2016

Paris abandoned assigned targets entirely. Instead every party submits its own NDC, on a five-year cycle, with a duty of progression — each successive NDC must represent the party's “highest possible ambition” and go beyond the last.3

The trade it made is explicit: universality bought at the price of bindingness. All Parties participate, and no Party's target is set externally. What binds is not the number — it is the procedure: you must submit, you must report, you must be reviewed, you must ratchet.

Two precisions that catch people out

1 — Annex I ≠ Kyoto Annex B. Paris abolished the operative consequence of the split — everyone now has an NDC, which is what Fig. 2.2 is about. The binary stopped determining obligations and became a fault line in the politics instead.

4

The shift that made corresponding adjustments necessary

This is the most valuable thing in this session. If Session 00 left you wondering why corresponding adjustments exist rather than just what they are, here is the answer — and it is structural, not technical.

Fig. 2.2 — Why Article 6.4 is not simply “CDM version 2”. Under Kyoto, crediting host countries had no targets, so an exported credit left nothing behind to be double-counted. Paris gave every country an NDC — and the same transaction became an accounting problem overnight.

Universality created the double-counting problem. Corresponding adjustments are the fix.

This one insight resolves several confusions at once: why the CDM needed replacing rather than renaming; why Article 6 took six years to negotiate; why “authorised” and “unauthorised” credits trade at different prices; and why a host country now has a real, quantified reason to refuse an export it would happily have waved through in 2010.

The consequence that catches people out — two grades of credit in one market

Because a corresponding adjustment is applied only when a host authorises a transfer, the same project can produce credits of two different grades: adjusted (authorised, usable against another country's NDC or a compliance scheme like CORSIA) and unadjusted (unauthorised, usable for voluntary claims but not counted against any state's target).

They are physically identical tonnes and legally different assets. That distinction did not exist before Paris, it is invisible in the project itself, and it is decided by a ministry — which is exactly why Otorisasi appears as a defined term in Perpres 110 (Pasal 1(27), p.5) rather than being left to contract.4

How Paris enforces without enforcement

There is no court, no fine, no trade sanction. The compliance mechanism under Article 15 is explicitly facilitative and non-punitive. What Paris relies on instead is a three-step loop: you pledge (Art. 4), you report and are reviewed (Art. 13), and the collective result is assessed publicly (Art. 14) in time to shape your next pledge.

Whether that is sufficient is the central open question about the whole regime — and it is a legitimate criticism, not a settled one. But note what it means for you practically: the transparency framework, not a penalty, is what makes a corresponding adjustment real. A country that fails to apply one is not fined; it is visible. Design your expectations accordingly.

7

Where Indonesia sits in all this

Then and now

Under Kyoto: Indonesia was non-Annex I — no emission target, and therefore a CDM host country. Credits generated here could be sold to Annex I buyers with no accounting consequence for Indonesia whatsoever.

Under Paris: Indonesia has an NDC like everyone else. The same export now costs it something measurable. Indonesia's structural position flipped from “free seller” to “seller with a stake” — and Perpres 110's authorization architecture is the domestic institutional response to exactly that flip.

Indonesia's Enhanced NDC is commonly cited at 31.89% unconditional and 43.20% conditional reduction against BAU by 2030. [?] — figures widely reported but the NDC document has not been opened; to be verified and cited to the submitted NDC in Session 07 rather than relied on here.

Why this reframes the “should we sell carbon abroad” debate

Under Kyoto, exporting credits was close to free money for a non-Annex I country: revenue in, no target to protect. The instinct that carbon export is straightforwardly good was formed in that era and is still widely held.

Under Paris the arithmetic reversed. Every authorised export is a tonne Indonesia must find somewhere else to stay on track — so carbon export is now a genuine trade-off between revenue and domestic target headroom, not a windfall. A government lens on Session 03 has to weigh both sides; a developer lens has to accept that the state has a rational reason to be restrictive that did not exist fifteen years ago.

Three lenses

Government

Your NDC is simultaneously a pledge, an accounting boundary, and a budget you can sell pieces of. Authorising exports raises revenue and shrinks headroom. The transparency framework is where that trade becomes visible to everyone — so the decision is public even when the negotiation is not.

Project developer

Whether your credit can cross a border is not a property of your project. It is a sovereign decision made after your project exists. Build the authorization question into your development timeline, not your sales process.

Project / asset owner

Post-Paris, “carbon credit” names two different assets — adjusted and unadjusted — with different buyers and different prices. Any model that assumes a single credit price is assuming an authorization decision it has not verified.

Anatomy of Article 6

“Article 6” is commonly used as shorthand for 6.2 and 6.4. The article has nine paragraphs; the others carry operative rules that determine how those two work in practice.

Fig. 3.1 — Article 6 structure. Cited by paragraph, the correct unit for treaty text.1

2

6.2 — cooperative approaches

The bilateral door. Two governments agree terms between themselves; the UN receives reports but issues nothing.

What 6.2 actually requires

The paragraph is short and its obligations are conditions, not procedures. Parties using ITMOs toward an NDC must promote sustainable development, ensure environmental integrity and transparency including in governance, and apply robust accounting to avoid double counting, consistent with CMA guidance.1

Article 6.3 adds the two words that give host countries their power: use of ITMOs is voluntary and authorized by participating Parties. That is the treaty basis for every national authorization regime, Indonesia's included.

The reporting chain — where “robust accounting” actually happens

6.2 has no supervisory body, so its discipline is reporting. A participating party submits an initial report setting out the cooperative approach and how it will avoid double counting; then regular and annual information on transfers; and reflects all of it in its Biennial Transparency Report under Article 13.2

Practical reading: 6.2 integrity is a function of who your counterparty government is. There is no central referee, so the quality of a 6.2 arrangement depends on the two parties' own standards and on the exposure that reporting creates. This is the reason 6.2 deals are fast and the reason they are scrutinised.

3

6.4 — the mechanism, now PACM

The centralised door, and the CDM's institutional heir. A UN body approves the methodology, accredits the auditor, and issues the unit.

What Article 6.4 sets up

A mechanism under the authority and guidance of the CMA, supervised by a designated body, with four stated aims: promote mitigation alongside sustainable development; incentivise participation by public and private entities authorised by a Party; contribute to emission reductions in the host Party which another Party may also use toward its NDC; and deliver an overall mitigation in global emissions.1

That fourth aim is what most sharply separates 6.4 from the CDM. The CDM moved a tonne from one ledger to another. 6.4 is required to shrink the total.

Two further paragraphs carry money and law: 6.5 bans the host from also counting a transferred reduction, and 6.6 requires a share of proceeds to fund administration and to help particularly vulnerable developing countries meet adaptation costs.

PACM — the name, the body, and the two levies

PACM — the Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism — is the operating name adopted for the Article 6.4 mechanism. Its supervisory body sets methodology and removals standards, accredits validation and verification bodies (the CDM's DOE role), registers activities and issues units.

The Glasgow rules attach two mandatory levies to 6.4 issuance: a share of proceeds for adaptation, and a mandatory cancellation of a portion of units to deliver OMGE. Commonly cited at 5% and 2% respectively. [?] — the design is certain; the exact percentages should be confirmed against the CMA decision text before you quote them in any deliverable. Flagged rather than asserted.

Under 6.2 neither is mandatory — they are encouraged. That asymmetry is a real commercial difference, not a technicality: a 6.4 unit arrives with a levy already deducted; a 6.2 unit need not.

What happened to the CDM pipeline

The transition rules permit eligible CDM activities to move into the 6.4 mechanism on host-country request within a deadline, and permit limited use of pre-existing CERs toward first NDCs, subject to conditions on vintage. [?] — the eligibility cut-offs and deadlines are specific and consequential; they are an explicit reading item for Session 04 and I will not paraphrase them from memory here.

The reason to care: a large legacy pipeline of registered CDM projects, Indonesian ones among them, sat in limbo for years pending these rules. Whether an old project can transition is a live commercial question with a documented answer — which we will read rather than guess.

The consequence for how a host country should think

If domestic abatement is cheap and the NDC is comfortably achievable, selling is attractive: you monetise headroom you did not need. If the NDC is tight and abatement is expensive, every export makes the remaining task harder and dearer — you are selling your cheapest tonnes and keeping the expensive ones.

So the rational sequencing question is: which tonnes can we afford to sell, and at what price does selling beat keeping? That is a marginal abatement cost question — Session 01's Fig. 1.1, applied to a national portfolio. The two sessions join here.

The article that makes the whole logic explicit — Pasal 56(2)

Sitting fifteen pages earlier is the provision that completes the picture. The Minister is obliged to count all emission reductions produced through GHG offset as part of Indonesia's NDC achievement — “sepanjang belum diterbitkan Corresponding Adjustment sesuai ketentuan UNFCCC” — for as long as no corresponding adjustment has been issued under UNFCCC provisions.3

Read that carefully, because it inverts the intuition most people start with. The default is that the tonne belongs to Indonesia. A corresponding adjustment is not what grants Indonesia the credit — it is what takes it away. Export is the exception that has to be actively authorised, not the natural state of a credit.

Resolved — LVV accreditation is by KAN, and the regulation names it

Session 00 flagged that Lembaga Validasi dan Verifikasi is absent from the Pasal 1 definitions, and I declined to assert KAN as the accreditor. The provisions are at Pasal 84–86, pp. 57–58.3

Pasal 84(2): validation and verification for entities undertaking Carbon Trading and Results-Based Payment is performed by an independent validation and verification body. 1. the Komite Akreditasi Nasional (KAN), for the SRUK carbon-unit issuance scheme; and/or

Resolved — where the offset-use limit lives

Session 01 described the “cut first, offset the residual” limit as a standard feature of compliance systems. Indonesia has it, at Pasal 62(1) huruf d, p.45: among the steps by which a sectoral minister runs emissions trading is setting the portion of the emission cap that may be compensated with GHG Offset.3

The mitigation hierarchy is therefore not rhetoric in Indonesian law — it is a quantitative dial, set per sector, by the sectoral minister.

7

Four more things the trading chapter reveals

Reading Pasal 55–68 for the Article 6 anchor surfaced several design choices worth carrying into later sessions.

1 — The carbon tax is the ETS enforcement mechanism

Pasal 63, p.45. A regulated installation must keep emissions under its cap, and may comply by mitigating, buying quota from another installation, or buying offsets. If it exceeds the cap anyway, it is obliged to pay carbon tax.3

This is the hybrid design from Session 01 made concrete — and it means the carbon tax rate functions as an effective price ceiling on the allowance market. No rational firm pays more than the tax for a quota. Whatever that rate is set at is, in practice, the maximum carbon price Indonesia's ETS can produce.

2 — Trading does not wait for the NDC

Pasal 58(1), p.43: “Perdagangan Karbon diselenggarakan tanpa menunggu tercapainya target NDC” — carbon trading is conducted without waiting for the NDC target to be met. A deliberate policy statement that the market is not gated on target achievement. Pasal 69(2), p.48 adds that domestic carbon trading may be linked to international carbon markets.

3 — Results-based payment transfers no ownership

Pasal 70(3), p.49: results-based payment does not cause a transfer of carbon ownership, and Pasal 70(4) keeps the achieved reductions inside Indonesia's NDC.

That is the cleanest possible statement of why RBP suits forestry: you are paid for the outcome and you keep the tonne. No corresponding adjustment, no headroom lost. Session 10 returns to this.

4 — SPE-GRK cannot be minted from spare allowances

Pasal 88(4), p.60: SPE-GRK cannot be issued from leftover emission quota.3 An integrity firewall between the two lanes of Session 00's Fig. 0.1: an installation that under-emits because its cap was generous cannot convert that slack into sellable credits. Unused allowance is not an achievement.

Also worth noting: Pasal 65(8), p.47 specifies that SRUK operates as a decentralised network whose data and transactions are transparent, traceable, real-time, permanent, and interoperable with other registries — unusually specific infrastructure language for a Presidential Regulation.

Three lenses on Article 6

Government

Authorization is your only point of control, and Pasal 68 places it with the Minister on sectoral recommendation. Every grant converts target headroom into revenue. The strategic question is not whether to authorise but which tonnes, at what price, in which sectors — a portfolio decision, not a permitting one.

Project developer

Your route is chosen at design stage and reaches down through every layer: DRAM or DPP → domestic or international standard → KAN-accredited or internationally accredited verifier → authorised or unauthorised. Pick the destination before you draft the document.

Project / asset owner

Under Pasal 68 the same tonne is two assets. Diligence question: has authorization been granted, is it applied for, or is it merely assumed? If the model prices credits at internationally authorised levels and the answer is “assumed”, the revenue line is unfounded.

Baseline — the rulebook gives exactly three routes

Session 00 introduced the baseline as the number that determines credit volume. Here is how Article 6.4 constrains it — and note how much stricter it is than the CDM instinct of “projected business as usual”.

Para. 33 sets the overarching quality bar for every methodology: it must be “real, transparent, conservative, credible, below ‘business as usual’”, avoid leakage, recognise suppressed demand, align to the Paris temperature goal, and contribute to reducing emission levels in the host Party.1

“Below business as usual” is the phrase that separates PACM from the CDM.

A CDM baseline aimed to represent business as usual. A 6.4 baseline must sit underneath it. Every credit is therefore discounted by construction — the mechanism is designed so that crediting alone tightens the counterfactual over time.

Why standardized baselines are the quiet efficiency win

Session 00 flagged MRV as the dominant recurring cost, and noted it scales badly for small projects. A standardized baseline moves the counterfactual argument from the project to the sector: it is settled once, centrally, for everyone. Each project then inherits it rather than paying consultants to re-derive it.

For Indonesia this is the highest-leverage thing a government can do for a small-project pipeline — industrial efficiency, small hydro, biogas — because it removes the fixed cost that makes those projects uneconomic to certify individually. Worth watching whether the implementing regulations take it up.

Additionality — the definition Session 00 could not find

Perpres 110 never defines it. The Article 6.4 rulebook does, in one dense paragraph, and it is stricter than the folk definition.

Para. 38 — “Additionality shall be demonstrated using a robust assessment that shows the activity would not have occurred in the absence of the incentives from the mechanism, taking into account all relevant national policies, including legislation, and representing mitigation that exceeds any mitigation that is required by law or regulation, and taking a conservative approach that avoids locking in levels of emissions, technologies or carbon-intensive practices…”1

The regulatory-surplus test is the one that bites in Indonesia

Because additionality must account for all relevant national policies, including legislation, an ambitious domestic policy destroys the additionality of projects that merely comply with it. If a regulation mandates a technology, deploying that technology can no longer be credited.

This creates a genuine and underappreciated tension for a government: the more you regulate, the smaller your creditable pipeline becomes. It is not a reason to regulate less — it is a reason to design carbon policy and sectoral regulation together, and to expect the creditable universe to shrink as regulation matures. Carry this into Session 10, where it directly shapes what is creditable in Indonesian power and industry.

The one flexibility the rulebook grants

Para. 39 permits the Supervisory Body to apply simplified additionality approaches for any least developed country or small island developing State at that Party's request. Indonesia is neither, so the standard demonstration applies — a small but real competitive asymmetry against LDC-hosted projects.

Renewal is a re-test, not a rubber stamp

Para. 57: renewing a crediting period requires approval by both the Supervisory Body and the host Party, following a technical assessment by a DOE to determine necessary updates to the baseline, the additionality and the quantification of emission reductions.

Removals get up to three times the crediting horizon, because a forest or a geological store must be watched for far longer than a turbine needs to be paid for. The same asymmetry appears in the obligations: para. 31(d)(ii) requires activities to minimise the risk of non-permanence over multiple NDC implementation periods and, where reversals occur, ensure these are addressed in full; para. 50 requires participants to monitor potential reversals beyond the reduction itself.

“Addressed in full” is an open-ended liability attached to a finite revenue stream.

An avoided tonne from a wind farm is done. A stored tonne in a forest is a promise that must survive fire, harvest and politics — and if it fails, the rulebook says it must be made good in full. That single clause is why forestry credits price lower than energy credits of identical vintage, and why buffer pools exist at all.

The CER carve-out — and why it is strange

Para. 75 lets CERs be used toward an NDC only if all of: the CDM activity was registered on or after 1 January 2013 (75(a)); the CERs are held in the mechanism registry and identified as pre-2021 reductions (75(b)); and they are used toward the first NDC only (75(c)). Paragraph 75(d) then departs from the pattern above: for these CERs the host Party is “not required to apply a corresponding adjustment” and they are not subject to the share of proceeds.

Read against Fig. 4.3, the treatment differs in two respects. A new 6.4 unit costs the host 100 tonnes of headroom plus a 5% levy. What makes an instrument “compliance”

One test, and it has nothing to do with trading.

A compliance instrument is one where failing to comply is a breach of law.

Not a broken promise, not a reputational problem — a legal violation with a legal consequence. Everything else about the instrument (whether anything is traded, whether a price or a quantity is fixed, whether a unit even exists) is design detail layered on top of that single fact.

This is why the ETS-shaped mental model misleads. An ETS happens to combine three things — a legal obligation, a quantity cap, and a tradable unit. Strip out the tradable unit and you have a carbon tax. Strip out the cap and you have a baseline-and-credit scheme. Strip out both and you have a performance standard. All four are compliance. Only one is an ETS.

Why baseline-and-credit keeps being chosen over cap-and-trade

A cap forces the government to decide, in advance and in public, the total tonnage the economy may emit — and then to allocate it, which means choosing winners. A facility-level baseline avoids both. Each installation gets its own line, obligations are calibrated to what that installation can plausibly do, and no aggregate number ever has to be defended in parliament.

The cost is exactly what Session 01 predicted: you lose the tonnage guarantee. If output grows, total emissions can grow while every facility complies. It is the intensity-versus-absolute trade, embedded in the choice of instrument rather than in the target.

3

Allocation — who captures the value a cap creates

The cap determines the environmental outcome. Allocation determines who receives the value that cap creates — which is why it attracts most of the lobbying.

A cap makes the right to emit scarce. Scarcity creates value — a scarcity rent. That value has to go somewhere, and the allocation method is nothing more than the decision about who receives it.

Grandfathering vs benchmarking — and why the difference is moral as well as technical

Grandfathering allocates in proportion to historical emissions. The dirtier you were, the more you receive. It is administratively simple, and it allocates most to the firms that emitted most, while giving no credit to anyone who cut emissions before the baseline year.

Benchmarking allocates in proportion to output at a performance standard — typically the average of the best performers in the sector, which is the same construction the Article 6.4 rulebook uses for an ambitious baseline (Session 04, ¶36(a)(ii)). Efficient producers receive more allowances than they need and can sell; laggards must buy. It rewards the right behaviour and it is much harder to compute.

The practical consequence for you: when an Indonesian sectoral benchmark is proposed, the question to ask is not “how tight?” but “benchmarked on what, against whom?” That single choice determines which firms in the sector are net buyers and which are net sellers — before a single tonne is abated.

4

The EU as reference case — what it actually teaches

Not because Indonesia should copy it, but because it is the only system with twenty years of mistakes on the public record.

What it is

Established in 2005, the first international emissions trading system, now in its fourth phase (2021–2030). It covers around 10,000 installations in the energy sector and manufacturing industry, plus aviation and maritime operators, across all Member States together with Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway.2

Gases and sources are more specific than “carbon”: CO₂ from electricity and heat generation and energy-intensive manufacturing such as steel and cement; N₂O from production of nitric, adipic and glyoxylic acids; and perfluorocarbons from aluminium production. Aviation covers flights within the European Economic Area and departing flights to Switzerland and the United Kingdom. From 2024 the system also covers maritime transport — 100% of emissions on voyages between EU ports and 50% on voyages starting or ending outside the EU.2

The Commission also notes its ETS pages are being updated following a July 2026 proposal, so anything time-sensitive should be checked against the current text.

The three lessons worth importing

1 — An over-allocated cap is worse than no cap. The EU's first phase issued more allowances than there were emissions to cover. The price collapsed, no abatement was induced, and the system spent a decade rebuilding credibility. A loose cap does not fail gracefully; it produces a functioning market that changes nothing while appearing to work.

2 — Surplus is structural, so quantity systems need a quantity valve. Recessions, cheap gas and overlapping renewable policies all cut emissions without cutting the cap, and the surplus persists for years because allowances bank. The EU's answer was the Market Stability Reserve: a quantity-side stabiliser. Session 01's hybrids, made necessary by experience rather than theory.

3 — Free allocation is easy to start and very hard to stop. It was introduced to protect trade-exposed industry from leakage; twenty years later, removing it required inventing an entirely new instrument at the border. Whatever Indonesia grants freely at the start of Phase 2 will be extraordinarily difficult to withdraw later. Design the exit before you grant the entry.

“Compliance vs market priority”, settled

Session 01 split this phrase into two debates and deferred both. Both can now be answered properly.

Reading A — the mitigation hierarchy, and how it is enforced

The principle is cut your own emissions first, offset only the residual. The enforcement is not exhortation — it is a quantitative cap on offset use inside the compliance obligation.

Indonesia has exactly this, and Session 03 located it: Pasal 62(1) huruf d, p.45 makes “setting the portion of the emission cap that may be compensated with GHG Offset” one of the enumerated steps by which a sectoral minister operates emissions trading.3 The hierarchy is a dial, set per sector, by the sectoral ministry.

Why the limit exists: offsets are usually cheaper than deep sectoral abatement. Uncapped, a rational regulated sector would buy the cheapest tonnes indefinitely and never rebuild its capital stock. The cap would be met on paper while the transition never happened. The offset limit is what forces the expensive, structural abatement to occur.

Reading B — which market a country builds first

Compliance-first creates guaranteed demand but needs legislation, MRV, a registry, a regulator and a fight with industry — years of work. Voluntary-first can be stood up quickly, but demand is discretionary, thin, and collapses when integrity is questioned.

The sequencing trap in voluntary-first: projects get financed against a demand curve that can evaporate, and the price expectations formed in the voluntary phase are inherited by the compliance system that follows.

Indonesia's answer is to refuse the sequence. Pasal 55(2), p.42 defines carbon trading, results-based payment and the carbon levy as one instrument family, not a phased programme; Pasal 58(1), p.43 states that carbon trading proceeds “tanpa menunggu tercapainya target NDC” — without waiting for the NDC target to be achieved.3

Leakage, and why the border became an instrument

The one problem that forces every compliance system to compromise — and the reason a seventh instrument type exists at all.

Carbon leakage is the migration of emissions rather than their reduction: a carbon price raises costs for domestic producers of internationally traded goods, production shifts to an unpriced jurisdiction, and global emissions are unchanged or worse while the pricing jurisdiction loses the industry. It is the argument behind every free-allocation concession ever granted.

There are only three answers, and every system uses some mix: exempt or freely allocate to exposed sectors (protects competitiveness, surrenders the rent and weakens the signal); price imports at the border so domestic and imported goods face the same carbon cost; or get everyone else to price carbon too, which returns you to the 28% figure above and to Article 6.

Why this is an Indonesian issue, not a European one

A border adjustment operates on the embedded carbon of imports. For an exporter of carbon-intensive goods, that converts another jurisdiction's climate policy into a direct cost on your own production — and it does so whether or not you have a domestic carbon price.

The strategic consequence is counter-intuitive and important: a domestic carbon price stops being purely a cost and starts being a way of keeping revenue at home. If the carbon cost of an exported tonne will be collected somewhere regardless, the live question becomes which treasury collects it. That reframing — carbon pricing as revenue retention rather than as burden — is one of the strongest arguments available to a finance ministry, and it changes the political economy of Session 01's instrument choice considerably.

What actually broke in 2023

The crisis is usually described in headlines. The underlying finding is more specific, and more useful, than the headlines were.

The most-cited peer-reviewed result is West et al. (2023) in Science, which examined REDD+ project sites and compared claimed avoided deforestation against independent counterfactual estimates.1

The evaluated projects claimed an aggregate 10.7 times more avoided deforestation than independent estimates justified. The discrepancy was not driven by the choice of forest-cover data, but by selection bias in the projects' control areas and in modelling approaches. The study covered 26 REDD+ project sites, of which 18 had sufficient public information on baselines.1

The mechanism behind the figure: the failure was in the counterfactual, exactly where Session 00 said it would be.

Nothing was miscounted. The forests were measured correctly. What was wrong was the comparison — the estimate of what would have happened otherwise. Baseline construction is where the discretion lives, so it is where the error concentrates. This is the same point the Article 6.4 rulebook is built around when it demands baselines be “conservative… below business as usual” and confines them to three approved approaches (Session 04, ¶33 and ¶36).

Being fair to the evidence — the picture is now more nuanced than 2023

The 2023 finding was contested by the standards concerned, and the literature has since moved. A subsequent Science paper is titled “Tropical forest carbon offsets deliver partial gains amid persistent over-crediting” — a framing that concedes real gains alongside persistent over-issuance rather than dismissing the category outright.2 [?] — that paper is paywalled and I could not open it, so I rely only on its title and assert none of its specific findings.

The state of the evidence: the direction of the finding is robust and repeatedly replicated — avoided-deforestation crediting has systematically over-issued — while the magnitude is contested and methodology-dependent. Anyone who tells you either “offsets are worthless” or “the critiques were debunked” is overstating what the evidence supports.

What can a buyer actually claim?

The question the bifurcation forces, and the one most corporate communications still get wrong.

Follow the accounting. A German company buys an unauthorised credit from an Indonesian project. No corresponding adjustment is applied. Under Perpres 110 Pasal 56(2) the Minister still counts that reduction toward Indonesia's NDC, because no CA has been issued.6 So the tonne is counted once — by Indonesia.

The German company's money paid for a reduction that counts toward Indonesia's target, not Germany's.

This is best described as climate finance: a private transfer that helps a developing country meet its own commitment. What it is not is the neutralisation of the buyer's own emissions. Describing it as offsetting misstates where the tonne is counted.

Indonesia's quiet advantage

Put Pasal 68(1)(b) and Pasal 56(2) together and Indonesia has legislated something rather clever.

Sell the credit, keep the tonne

Pasal 68(1) huruf b permits cross-border offset trading without Otorisasi or a corresponding adjustment, provided the units are not used for any NDC or other international obligation. Pasal 56(2) then keeps those reductions inside Indonesia's own NDC accounting.6

The combined effect: Indonesia can host voluntary-market projects, let foreign money finance them, permit the credits to be sold abroad — and still count every tonne toward its own NDC. Foreign capital, domestic target progress, no headroom surrendered.

The price is that those credits are the lower-value tier and support only a contribution claim. So the policy question is a genuine trade, not a free lunch: authorise and earn more per tonne while losing headroom, or withhold authorisation and earn less while keeping the tonne. Perpres 110 gives the Minister that dial, per transaction, on sectoral recommendation.

Why forestry dominates Indonesian voluntary issuance — and energy does not

Three reinforcing reasons, all of which have now been established from primary text rather than assumed:

1 — Instrument fit. Session 01's table: forestry produces removals with reversal risk, dispersed counterparties and land-rights questions. Correction — the carbon tax is not a price ceiling

Correcting Session 05, §6 and Fig. 5.3

I said the carbon tax rate caps Indonesia's allowance price. The statute says the opposite.

In Session 05 I reasoned that because Perpres 110 Pasal 63(3) makes exceeding your cap trigger an obligation to pay carbon tax, no rational firm would ever pay more than the tax rate for a quota — so the tax rate is an effective price ceiling. That is the standard hybrid design, and I applied it without reading UU HPP Pasal 13. I should have read it first.

The Directorate General of Taxes states the rule: the carbon tax rate is set equal to or higher than the carbon market price per kilogram CO₂e, and where the market price falls below Rp30,00 per kg CO₂e, the rate is set at a minimum of Rp30,00.2

What I said

Tax rate is fixed → paying tax is cheaper than a quota above that level → the tax caps the market price.

What the law provides

Tax rate tracks the market upward and never sits below it → paying tax is never cheaper than complying → the tax is a floor under compliance, not a ceiling on price.

Why it matters, and why the error was substantive rather than cosmetic: a price ceiling weakens a cap — it caps the abatement signal. A rate pegged at or above the market does the reverse: it removes the option of buying your way out cheaply, so the cap can bind as hard as the government sets it. Indonesia's design is stronger than I described, not weaker.

The Rp30,00/kg floor is Rp30,000 per tonne CO₂e — low by international standards [?] (I am not converting to dollars; the rate depends on an exchange rate I have not fixed to a source). But it is a floor, and it only binds when the market is weaker still.

I reached the wrong answer by reasoning correctly from a generic model of hybrid instruments instead of reading the specific statute. That is exactly the failure mode this curriculum's sourcing discipline exists to catch — and it is the failure mode you should expect from any adviser, consultant or briefing note that describes Indonesia's system by analogy to the EU's.

The regulation reaches the function only obliquely: Pasal 65(3)(c), p.46 requires a DRAM or DPP to state its “application of national, UNFCCC, and other international methodology and standards.” Additionality therefore enters Indonesian practice through whichever methodology a project adopts — which for an international-standard route means the Article 6.4 test at ¶38, read in Session 04, or a voluntary standard's equivalent.

Where it will be written down domestically is signposted twice: Pasal 34, p.31 sends the detailed mitigation procedures to a Peraturan Menteri, and Pasal 102, p.67 requires all implementing regulations to be established within one year.

The test that determines whether a project earns credits is not set out in the Presidential Regulation. It is in ministerial rules that were not yet published when this regulation took effect.

That is not unusual drafting — a Perpres sets architecture. But it does mean that anyone telling you today what qualifies as additional in Indonesia is either citing a ministerial regulation you should ask to see, or is extrapolating. Ask which.

The frame Indonesia uses for all of this

Worth knowing, because it explains design choices that otherwise look merely bureaucratic.

The Ministry of Forestry presents Perpres 110 as strengthening state sovereignty over carbon, grounding it in Article 33 of the 1945 Constitution — natural resources are controlled by the state for the greatest prosperity of the people — and stating that carbon economic value management sits under the full control of the Government of Indonesia. The stated ambition is Indonesian carbon credits that are world-class, high-integrity and globally recognised, without setting aside national development priorities.3

The authorisation provisions can be read as an expression of that constitutional claim rather than as administrative process. analysis

Every gate this curriculum has catalogued — Otorisasi at Pasal 68(2), Menteri Terkait approval even on the international-standard route at Pasal 65(6), mandatory SRUK recording at Pasal 59(1) — is an expression of the same constitutional claim: carbon is a national resource, and the state decides the terms on which it leaves. Read as export control rather than as permitting steps, the sequence and its timing become easier to anticipate.

Industrial energy efficiency — the squeeze

Your third energy sub-sector, and the one where the mechanisms interact most awkwardly.

Three constraints closing in on the same projects

1 — The regulatory-surplus test. Decision -/CMA.3 ¶38 requires creditable mitigation to exceed what law or regulation already requires. 2 — Negative-cost measures need more than a price. The cheapest abatement in your sector is the hardest to monetise through carbon markets.

Industrial energy efficiency is where the negative-cost tonnes are — and it is simultaneously the most exposed to the additionality squeeze, the least responsive to a price signal, and administratively split from the power scheme. Expect the compliance market to deliver less efficiency abatement than the MACC suggests is available, and treat efficiency policy as a regulatory and finance problem with a carbon-market component, rather than the reverse.

Indonesia tightened its own forest baseline, which is the opposite of the incentive Session 06 found operating in project-level REDD+.

Why forestry sits in VCM and results-based payment rather than the ETS

Session 01 gave the structural reasons and every one of them now has an Indonesian citation. Forestry produces removals with reversal risk — and Indonesia's own emissions history proves the point: the NDC reports 2019 emissions dominated by LUCF including peat fires at 50.13%, against 2022 emissions dominated by energy at 69.01%. The difference is largely fire. A cap-and-trade obligation cannot sensibly be placed on an asset whose emissions swing that hard on weather.

Counterparties are dispersed, with land-tenure and community-rights questions an allowance market has no way to handle. And Pasal 70(3) gives the decisive institutional answer: results-based payment transfers no carbon ownership, and under Pasal 70(4) the reductions stay in Indonesia's NDC. For a sector that must deliver a 389,796 Gg swing, that is exactly the instrument you want — payment without surrender.

[?] Whether forestry in fact dominates Indonesian voluntary-market issuance — flagged in Session 06 — remains unverified; I have not pulled registry data. The structural argument stands regardless of the issuance split.

The project

Deliberately ordinary, and deliberately in your sector. Nothing exotic — which is the point, because the gates are the same for anything.

A textile manufacturer in Central Java

A privately-held spinning and weaving plant. Two measures, one project: 20 MWp of captive solar across rooftop and adjacent land, and a motor, drive and compressed-air upgrade across the production line. Expected abatement in the order of 25,000 tCO₂e per year, from displaced grid electricity and reduced consumption.

Everything below is hypothetical — a vehicle for tracing the mechanisms. No figure here is a claim about any real project, and none should be lifted into a model.

Before any of the carbon machinery: this plant is in Central Java, which Session 10's data shows is Indonesia's largest electricity-producing province — on a grid that is 67.5% coal.

That is why displaced grid power is worth counting at all. The emission factor behind these 25,000 tonnes is a coal-heavy grid. In a system dominated by hydro, the same 20 MWp would abate a fraction of this. The value of the project is a property of the grid it sits on, not of the panels.

The gates, in order

Nine of them. Two are forks that change what the asset becomes; one is where most projects die.

Gate 1 — coveragePerpres 110 Pasal 62 · Permen ESDM 16/2022

Is this an Instalasi yang Diatur?

No. Phase I of the power-sector scheme covers coal plants ≥100 MW on the PLN grid. A textile plant is not a power plant, and the industry sub-sector falls to Menteri Perindustrian under Pasal 24(2)(b) — a scheme for which I found no evidence of operation. [?]

Consequence: no cap, no quota, no surrender obligation, no carbon tax backstop. This project cannot sell allowances because it was never given any. It goes down the offset route at Pasal 65 — the path for entities outside the regulated set.

Gate 2 — additionalityCMA.3 annex ¶38 · PP 33/2023← where projects die

Does it exceed what the law already requires?

Two limbs, and the project splits on them.

The motors and compressors: PP No. 33/2023 mandates energy management above a consumption threshold, minimum energy performance standards and efficiency labelling. If the upgrade merely brings equipment to MEPS, ¶38's requirement that mitigation “exceeds any mitigation that is required by law or regulation” is not met. Those tonnes are compliance, not credit.

The solar: not mandated, so regulatory surplus is satisfied. But the counterfactual limb bites instead — ¶38 requires showing the activity would not have occurred without the incentive from the mechanism. If captive solar already pays back on displaced tariff alone, the honest answer may be that it would have happened anyway.

The realistic outcome: a project pitched at 25,000 tCO₂e may be creditable for materially less. This is a recognised source of divergence between a developer's projected volume and a validator's conclusion. It reflects the test operating as drafted rather than a procedural obstacle.

Gate 3 — the first forkPerpres 110 Pasal 65(2)a, (3)c · p.46

DRAM or DPP?

Submit a DRAM — meeting Government requirements, targeting SPE-GRK — or a DPP — meeting an international standard, targeting non-SPE-GRK units. Either is filed with Menteri Terkait, here Perindustrian, for recording.

This is chosen before validation, before construction. It determines the standard, the registry, the accreditation route, the buyer pool and the price. Session 00 called it a market strategy disguised as a form; it determines the standard, registry, accreditation route, buyer pool and price.

Gate 4 — validationPasal 65(2)b · Pasal 85(1)c · p.58

Who is allowed to check you

A Lembaga Validasi Independen assesses the DRAM or DPP. Accreditation follows the Gate 3 fork exactly: KAN for the SRUK domestic scheme; an internationally valid accreditation body for the international-standard route. Competence standards sit in a KAN regulation (Pasal 85(2)); procedures in a Peraturan Menteri Terkait (Pasal 86).

Gates 5–6 — build, monitor, verifyPasal 65(2)c–e · CMA.3 ¶50–51

The years in the middle

Implement per the DRAM/DPP; monitor; then a Lembaga Verifikasi Independen verifies the achieved reductions and the results are reported to Menteri Terkait. Under Pasal 78, the achievement is Baseline minus actual — the subtraction from Session 00, now with a named regulator on each side of it.

Metering design decided at engineering stage determines whether this is cheap or ruinous. Decide it before you pour concrete.

Gate 7 — issuancePasal 65(5),(6),(7) · pp.46–47

Who puts the unit into existence

SPE-GRK route: the Menteri (environment) issues, on the recommendation of Menteri Terkait. Non-SPE-GRK route: the international standard issues — but only after the stages above and with the approval of Menteri Terkait. Either way the units are recorded in SRUK.

Note what that means: even on the international route, an Indonesian ministry holds a consent. There is no path to a marketable Indonesian credit that does not pass through the state.

Gate 8 — the second forkPasal 68(1),(2) · pp.47–48 · Pasal 56(2) · p.42

Authorised, or not?

The sorting question is not “is this a Paris credit?” but “is the unit being used to meet an obligation?” — another country's NDC, or any other international obligation such as CORSIA. If yes, it needs Otorisasi from the Menteri on Perindustrian's recommendation, plus a corresponding adjustment. If no, Pasal 68(1)(b) requires neither.

And under Pasal 56(2) the default runs the other way from most people's intuition: the Minister must count these reductions toward Indonesia's NDC for as long as no corresponding adjustment has been issued. Export is the exception that must be actively granted, not the natural state of a credit.

Gate 9 — salePasal 58(2), 59 · POJK 14/2023

Exchange or direct

Trading may be through the Bursa Karbon or by direct trade (Pasal 58(2)); every trade must be recorded in SRUK (Pasal 59(1)). If it goes across IDXCarbon, the unit is legally a security and the whole OJK apparatus attaches — licensing, market conduct, settlement, disclosure.

You are managing a budget, not running a permitting office

Ministry of Environment · sectoral ministries · Ministry of Finance · steering committee

The developer lens, in full

Your revenue is a policy artifact, and your route is chosen on day one

Project development · methodology · validation · offtake
  1. Settle additionality before you settle anything else. Check what PP 33/2023 and applicable MEPS already require, then ask honestly whether the project needs carbon revenue to happen. Gate 2 is where projects die, and it is cheaper to die there than after validation.
  2. DRAM or DPP is a market decision, not a filing decision. It fixes your standard, registry, accreditation route, buyer pool and price — and it is made before construction.
  3. Engage two ministries, not one. The sectoral ministry recommends issuance and sets the offset limit that creates your domestic demand; the environment ministry issues and authorises. Either alone leaves you exposed.
  4. Design metering at engineering stage. MRV is the dominant recurring cost and it scales badly for small projects. The decision that determines it is made by engineers, years before anyone thinks about carbon.
  5. Budget for the credit stream to shrink legitimately. Two independent mechanisms do this: Perpres 110 Pasal 18 lets the state revise the baseline on enumerated grounds, and CMA.3 ¶57 makes crediting-period renewal a genuine reassessment of baseline, additionality and quantification.
  6. Do not assume authorisation. It is a sovereign judgement about national headroom, made after your project exists, on grounds that have nothing to do with your project's quality. Build the timeline around it rather than the sales process.

The owner and investor lens, in full

Six questions before you underwrite a carbon revenue line

Diligence · valuation · risk
  1. “Which NDC is this model built against?” Anything quoting 31.89% below BAU is working from a superseded framework. The Second NDC is a level-of-emission target against a 2019 reference.
  2. “Is it authorised, applied for, or assumed?” The same tonne is two assets at two prices. A model priced at authorised levels on an unauthorised asset is out by the whole spread.
  3. “How many units do we actually receive?” Under a 6.4 route, ≤93% after the 5% adaptation levy and the minimum 2% OMGE cancellation — while the host applies a corresponding adjustment on all 100%.

Ten findings, each sourced

Compiled from eleven sessions. Each is recorded in the source ledger, and several cut against a common assumption.

A carbon market does not reduce emissions. The cap does. The market reduces the cost of the reduction. Judge a market by whether the cap binds, not by whether the market functions.

Corresponding adjustments exist because the world changed, not because the rules tightened. Under Kyoto, host countries had no targets, so an exported credit left nothing to double-count. Paris gave everyone an NDC and made the same transaction an accounting problem overnight.

In Indonesian law the tonne belongs to Indonesia by default. Pasal 56(2) requires the Minister to count offset reductions toward the NDC until a corresponding adjustment is issued. Export is the actively-granted exception.

The host applies a corresponding adjustment on 100% of issued A6.4ERs but is paid for at most 93%. The adaptation levy and the OMGE cancellation come out of national headroom too, and almost no revenue model shows it.

Additionality's hardest limb is regulatory surplus, not the counterfactual. Creditable mitigation must exceed what law already requires — so the more a country regulates, the smaller its creditable pipeline becomes.

Indonesia's carbon tax is a floor under compliance, not a ceiling on price. UU HPP pegs the rate at or above the market, so paying tax is never cheaper than complying. I had this backwards until I read the statute.

Indonesia's power ETS allocates by intensity, so the cap grows with output. A plant can emit substantially more, improve nothing, and remain fully compliant. The instrument improves coal-fleet efficiency; it does not shrink the fleet.

Indonesia's climate target is a forestry target with an energy problem attached. Energy emissions rise 69% to 2035 even in the ambitious scenario; FOLU swings 389,796 Gg from source to sink and carries the whole number.

Indonesia can sell a voluntary credit abroad and keep the tonne. Pasal 68(1)(b) plus Pasal 56(2) permit export without authorisation while the reduction still counts toward the NDC. The buyer gets a contribution claim, not an offsetting one — and that is honest, not deficient.

Nearly three quarters of global emissions still face no carbon price. About 28% are covered. That is the honest state of the field after three decades, and it is why Article 6 exists commercially.

Flag closed — additionality is now defined

Flagged in Session 00. Searched for and not found in Session 03. Confirmed definitively absent from the whole Perpres in Session 08. Here it is.

Pasal 1 angka 29. “Additionality adalah prinsip bahwa pengurangan atau penyerapan emisi dari suatu proyek karbon sebagai tambahan dibandingkan kondisi yang akan terjadi tanpa proyek tersebut.” Additionality is the principle that emission reduction or absorption from a carbon project is additional compared with the condition that would occur without that project.

And it is not merely definitional. Pasal 12 ayat (2) huruf b makes Additionality a required element of the supporting data accompanying a DRAM registration application.1 A project cannot register without addressing it.

What the Indonesian definition contains, and what it does not.

It states the counterfactual limb cleanly: additional compared with what would have happened without the project. It does not restate the Article 6.4 rulebook's other two limbs — that mitigation must exceed what law or regulation already requires, and that a conservative approach must avoid locking in carbon-intensive practices (Decision -/CMA.3 ¶38, Session 04).

That does not mean regulatory surplus is irrelevant in Indonesia — Pasal 12 ayat (1) huruf c requires the DRAM to state its application of national, UNFCCC and other international methodology and standards, and any Article 6.4 or major voluntary methodology carries the fuller test. But the domestic definition itself is narrower than the treaty one, and if you are advising on an Indonesian forestry project, that gap is worth knowing precisely.

2

What the forestry rule actually does

It operationalises the Pasal 65 offset route for forestry

The architecture is exactly what Session 11's capstone traced, now with forestry-specific plumbing. Pasal 10 confirms the two-unit structure: Unit Karbon is either SPE-GRK issued by the environment minister, or non-SPE-GRK issued by an international standard — the former on the recommendation of the Forestry Minister, the latter with the Forestry Minister's approval.1

Pasal 11 ayat (3) routes applications through SRUK. Pasal 12 sets out DRAM contents — mitigation plan, methodology and standards applied, environmental impact analysis, sustainable development impact analysis, public consultation, and supporting data including additionality and community-involvement plans.

It puts numbers on the forestry offset target

Pasal 4 ayat (1) huruf b requires the carbon trading roadmap to set offset targets of at least 48.69 million hectares of forest for emission-reduction mitigation actions, and at least 3.5 million hectares of critical or degraded land for absorption actions.1

Set that against Session 07: the NDC requires FOLU to swing 389,796 Gg from source to sink by 2035. These hectare figures are the operational expression of the sector that carries the national target.

It defines who may sell forest carbon

Pasal 6 lists eligible Pelaku Usaha: holders of PBPH (forest utilisation business licences) and management rights; holders of Social Forestry Management Approval; adat law communities holding customary forest status; private-forest registration holders; and holders of PB-PJL Karbon — a carbon environmental-services business licence for conservation areas and hunting parks.

Notably, Pasal 6 ayat (2) requires social forestry groups, adat communities and private-forest holders to be accompanied by a registered partner or facilitator. That is a real answer to the counterparty-dispersion problem Session 01 identified as the structural reason forestry fits poorly in an allowance market.

Nesting is the concept that resolves a problem this brief has circled since Session 03.

If Indonesia credits at national level (the NDC), at provincial level (jurisdictional programmes), and at project level, the same tonne can be claimed three times without anyone lying. Nesting is the discipline that stops it — and its appearance as a defined term, alongside Program Berbasis Yurisdiksi, signals that Indonesia intends to run jurisdictional and project crediting simultaneously. That is technically demanding and it is where forest-carbon integrity disputes concentrate globally.

Energy — still a draft, and broader than expected

Your primary sector has not yet received its rule. What is known about its intended shape is worth having.

Scope, as described by ESDM

The Director General of New and Renewable Energy and Energy Conservation, Eniya Listiani Dewi, described a draft Permen ESDM governing the business process for carbon economic value in the energy sector, still in internal synchronisation, with data integration across oil and gas, electricity, and minerals and coal outstanding before signature.2

Reported coverage is wider than the current power-only scheme: manufacturing industry energy use, buildings, transport including electric vehicles, carbon capture and storage, and waste-to-energy. Regulations were reported as targeted for completion by end-2026.

[?] All of this is press reporting of ministry statements, not a document. Nothing here should be treated as settled, and none of it tells you what happens to Permen ESDM 16/2022 — which remains the operative power-sector rule until something replaces it.

On sequencing: forestry got its rule first.

The sector that carries the national target and holds the credits international buyers want has an operative implementing regulation. The sector where emissions are planned to grow 69% is still synchronising spreadsheets. Whatever the reason, the practical effect is that Indonesian forest carbon has a working legal pathway right now, and industrial energy carbon does not.

6

Where the market is

Two datapoints from the same reporting, both worth holding loosely.

Price ambition versus price reality

The government is reported to be targeting a carbon price of up to US$40 per tonne CO₂, against a current level described as around US$4–5 per tonne.3

Two things follow. First, a roughly eightfold gap between ambition and market is not closed by exhortation — it is closed by a binding cap, which returns you to Session 10: an intensity benchmark that grows with output does not produce scarcity. Second, at US$4–5 the Rp30,000/tonne carbon tax floor from Session 08 sits in broadly the same range, meaning the statutory floor is currently doing real work rather than sitting far below the market. [?] — that comparison depends on an exchange rate I have not fixed to a source; treat it as an order-of-magnitude observation, not a calculation.

Also reported: ESDM is working on assigning carbon unit ownership rights directly to the developer or energy producer, so that firms investing in low-emission technology can claim the economic value themselves. If that holds, it is directly relevant to your owner lens — but it is a ministry statement about a draft, not a rule.

Three amendments to what earlier sessions said
